# Case Index

This index tracks case maturity and evidence type. A checked box in the roadmap is not enough: each case should show **what kind of claim has actually been established**.

## Status levels

### `scouted`

A potentially useful case has been identified, but its historical vocabulary and mechanism have not yet been verified.

### `first-pass`

The case has:

- a bounded research question;
- at least one credible historical or technical source;
- a mechanism-level description;
- explicit separation of historical record from modern analogy;
- known evidence gaps.

It is usable for comparison but should not be treated as settled.

### `grounded`

The case has:

- strong primary evidence where available;
- precise source locations;
- historical vocabulary;
- mechanism and failure modes;
- counterexamples / limits;
- related-repository duplication checked.

### `mature`

The case is strong enough to support synthesis across cases. It has survived source deepening and conceptual comparison, and its central claims no longer depend on a single source or fragile analogy.

## Evidence labels

Use these labels in case claim ledgers where useful:

| Label | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `H` | historical record |
| `P` | primary / contemporary evidence |
| `S` | scholarly or institutional secondary evidence |
| `E` | engineering / operational reconstruction |
| `A` | functional analogy |
| `I` | philosophical / media-theoretical interpretation |
| `X` | rejected or explicitly unsupported claim |

A claim may have more than one label, for example `H/P` or `E/A`.

## Cross-case findings already supported

After sixty-four bounded cases, **all sixty-four cases are now `grounded`.** The repository satisfies both the numeric and mechanism-variety gates for bounded synthesis. 2. **retention mechanism ≠ apparent persistence** — an abacus configuration can sit still, a delay-line pattern survives by continual circulation, a core can remain magnetized at rest, a DRAM cell survives for a bounded interval before scheduled regeneration, mapped Flash can preserve a logical object while relocating its physical embodiment, and RADOS can preserve an object while replica membership changes;

6. **historical material operation and modern reconstruction must remain layered** — early Chinese procedural texts establish placement/position language, while exact counting-rod manipulation sometimes remains a specialist reconstruction rather than a directly described artifact operation;
7. **cross-cultural functional similarity ≠ genealogy** — Cheng 1592 and Ries 1525 can support a shared mechanism comparison without proving transmission, common origin, or modern-register ancestry;
8. **identity of logical state ≠ identity of physical token** — delay-line regeneration, destructive-read core restore, DRAM regeneration, mapped Flash, and RADOS recovery all preserve logical sameness through changed physical state;
9. **idle nonvolatility ≠ read invariance** — the grounded magnetic-core evidence shows that an element can retain remanent state without maintenance energy yet the bounded classic read operation can destroy that state and require rewrite;
10. **access can itself create a retention obligation** — classic destructive-read core and Dennard's bounded 1T1C embodiment may require immediate rewrite after read;
11. **destructive read is a regime, not an essence of magnetic core** — contemporary 1953–1954 nondestructive sensing/readout work bounds the classic destructive-read case and rejects `all core reads are destructive`;
12. **time can itself create a retention obligation** — DRAM adds a distinct deadline: charge leakage requires scheduled regeneration even without useful access;
13. **dynamic retention ≠ destructive read** — Dennard disclosed nondestructive alternatives; Intel 1103 manufacturer documentation combines dynamic storage and periodic refresh with nondestructive read; AMD's 1976 Am9050 directly combines a one-transistor/capacitor cell, nondestructive read, and mandatory refresh;

17. **maintenance can be space/reclaim-triggered** — a nonvolatile medium may still require deferred copying and erasure so repeated logical rewrites can continue;
18. **reclamation ≠ wear leveling** — reclamation recovers writable capacity while preserving current data; Wells's 1992-lineage wear-leveling patent adds the distinct objective of distributing switching/erase burden to extend usable medium life;
19. **historical terminology must follow the source** — Ban's 1993-filed system uses `virtual map` / logical-unit vocabulary; Intel AP-619 documents `Flash Translation Layer (FTL)` as a PCMCIA-approved format by August 1995. Earlier mechanisms should not be retroactively renamed without evidence;
20. **replica multiplicity ≠ retained currentness** — several physical copies may exist while only a version-consistent subset represents the current ordered state;
21. **maintenance can be failure/repair-triggered** — distributed redundancy can degrade after failure or membership change and be restored by reconstructing current state onto replacement members; bounded 1998 NAND evidence also shows block replacement as a local device-management response to program/erase failure;
22. **logical success ≠ durable commit** — the bounded 2006 RADOS design distinguishes replicated in-memory acknowledgement from later persistent-media commit, and the contemporaneous 2007 dissertation preserves this distinction across the expanded replication discussion;

25. **refresh can be shared reconstruction, not merely a timer event** — commercial DRAM documentation makes row selection, sense amplification, restoration, and return-to-cell part of maintaining a large array of minimal storage cells;
26. **retention ≠ addressability** — a state can remain physically present while the selector, map, convention, or index needed to choose it is absent; passive positional retention can also be operationally selected by a human without autonomous machine addressing;
27. **address stability ≠ location stability** — DRAM preserves a stable cell-selection relation across repeated reconstruction, while mapped Flash and RADOS preserve higher-level designations across deliberate physical relocation or replica replacement;
28. **address resolution ≠ currentness authorization** — mapped Flash can leave an old embodiment physically present after logical invalidation, and RADOS can resolve a reachable replica that is stale or not currently authorized to answer;

30. **addressability is not one historical scalar** — human spatial selection, coordinate decoding, logical translation, and distributed placement are different operational relations with different costs; the repository should not narrate them as a simple monotonic ascent from `less` to `more` addressable;

132. **electrical programmability ≠ electrical erasability** — the bounded Intel/Frohman lineage electrically programs the floating gate but uses X-ray/UV discharge for erase; later EEPROM/Flash electrical-erase mechanisms must be separately sourced rather than projected backward;
133. **historical `read-only` ≠ physical unalterability** — the period phrase `electrically programmable read only memory` names a normal read-service role that coexists with separate programming and erase interventions; ROM vocabulary must be interpreted through the sourced operating regime;
134. **invention priority must be mechanism-specific** — Kahng's 1967-filed Bell Labs patent already describes trapped-charge floating-gate memory, and Frohman's own 1970-filed patent cites the Kahng/Sze work. The Intel sources ground a practical avalanche-injection/radiation-discharge design and decoded programmable array, not a universal `first floating-gate memory` claim;
135. **electrical erasure ≠ ordinary read-service equivalence** — Intel's 2816 brings erase into the electrical/in-system control path, yet READ, BYTE ERASE, BYTE WRITE, CHIP ERASE, and inhibit remain separate modes; erase/write uses an elevated 21 V VPP and millisecond-scale timing rather than the ordinary lower-voltage read path;
136. **electrical erasability ≠ one erase geometry** — the same 2816 supports selected byte erase without affecting adjacent bytes and a separate whole-chip erase mode; `electrical erase` therefore does not determine a unique granularity of forgetting;
137. **electrical erasability ≠ erase-free overwrite** — Intel's 2816 write procedure requires the selected byte to be erased before the intended data is written, so deliberate forgetting can become a constitutive stage of update even when both stages are electrically controlled;
138. **nonvolatility ≠ unlimited mutability** — the 2816 retains state without periodic refresh but is specified for applications requiring up to `1 × 10^4` erase/write cycles per byte; quiescent durability and repeated rewrite endurance are different properties;
139. **value-history forgetting ≠ physical stress-history irrelevance** — the 2816 does not retain an application-visible log of prior byte values, yet repeated erase/write events consume a finite material cycling budget and therefore continue to matter to future state-change reliability;
140. **product operating semantics ≠ exact patent embodiment or invention priority** — the 2816 manufacturer document grounds the named product's FLOTOX/byte/chip-erase behavior while US4203158A grounds a contemporary Intel tunneling mechanism class; neither source justifies silently declaring the patent drawing the exact 2816 production cell, and the patent's own prior-art discussion blocks a universal `Intel invented EEPROM` claim;
141. **program addressability ≠ erase addressability** — Intel's 1988-filed Flash design uses X/Y address and data paths for programming while its erase generator applies the erase condition to the entire array; the unit that can be selected for creation/change need not be the unit that must be forgotten together;
142. **electrical erasability ≠ fine-grained erasability** — Case 12 grounds byte-level electrical erase in the Intel 2816, while Case 13 grounds a whole-array electrical-erase Flash regime; moving erase into electrical control does not determine the scope of erase authority;
143. **fast bulk erase ≠ fast arbitrary rewrite** — Kynett et al.'s 1988 indexed abstract reports whole-array erase on the order of 200 ms and byte programming on the order of 100 µs typical; changing one logical value can still require preservation/reconstruction of other current values sharing the erase domain;
144. **coarse physical state change ≠ coarse verification granularity** — Intel's whole-array erase algorithm walks addresses afterward to verify erased state, repeats erase pulses when necessary, and can report bounded failure; collective state change and local proof of success are separate relations;
145. **one-transistor density objective can be coupled to shared erase authority without becoming a universal law** — Toshiba's 1980-priority patent explicitly contrasts a two-transistor E²PROM density penalty with a one-transistor electrically erasable cell and shared erase/control infrastructure, while its own alternative groupings and cited prior art prevent universalizing that arrangement;
146. **erase asymmetry ≠ FTL** — the early Flash sources establish a physical/control mismatch between fine read/program selection and coarse erase, creating the retention problem to which later copy/reclaim/remap systems respond; they do not establish a Flash Translation Layer, which remains historically separate in Case 04;
147. **logical-block identity ≠ physical-sector identity** — Chan's 1990-filed account explicitly distinguishes the host LBA from the physical target and shows grown-defect replacement redirecting the same logical block to a spare sector; a stable designation need not imply one permanent disk-surface location;
148. **reassignment continuity ≠ payload continuity** — Seagate's `REASSIGN BLOCKS` changes the physical medium serving an LBA but explicitly does not preserve the affected block's data; address continuity and value continuity must therefore be audited separately;
149. **logical-address preservation can constrain repair strategy** — Chan's account rejects renumbering already-used subsequent LBAs because it would cause lost data or misreads, motivating spare substitution while preserving the host-visible address sequence;
150. **defect metadata can be constitutive retention state** — PDL/SDL or equivalent replacement state participates in resolving an LBA to the current physical target and is itself loaded from disk at startup in the bounded Chan account;
151. **repair capacity can be finite hidden infrastructure** — Seagate permits repeated reassignment of one LBA across multiple physical addresses only until spare locations are exhausted; `NO DEFECT SPARE LOCATION AVAILABLE` turns reserved spare space into an explicit continuation limit;
152. **LBA abstraction ≠ disappearance of physical geometry or equivalence to FTL** — period disk sources retain physical target / cylinder-head-sector defect descriptions below a logical-block interface, and the defect-triggered spare substitution in this case remains historically and operationally distinct from Case 04's erase-driven Flash relocation/reclamation;
153. **nonvolatile medium ≠ every necessary current device state is already nonvolatile** — Intel's SSD 320 design explicitly places user/system data in temporary buffers as well as NAND, so the nonvolatility of the final medium does not erase a volatile durability window inside the device;
154. **explicit flush can define a volatile → nonvolatile completion boundary without defining every write semantic** — the 2007 ATA8-ACS working draft requires successful `FLUSH CACHE` completion only after volatile-cache data reach nonvolatile media or an error occurs, while the case deliberately avoids inventing a stronger ordinary-write acknowledgement rule;
155. **explicit flush ≠ orderly-shutdown handoff ≠ unexpected-power-loss emergency handoff** — ATA `FLUSH CACHE`, Intel's `STANDBY IMMEDIATE` shutdown path, and the SSD 320 power-fail interrupt/capacitor path can all move state toward NAND, but their triggers, authority, and failure assumptions differ;
156. **stored energy ≠ stored payload, while stored energy can still be retention infrastructure** — the SSD 320's power-loss-protection capacitance retains energy, not user data, yet that finite energy reserve is constitutive of finishing a transfer into NAND after input power begins to collapse;
157. **payload programming ≠ mapping/currentness durability** — Case 04 establishes mapping as retained state and FAST '13 supplies an independent controller-level counterexample in which programmed payload and validity/mapping consistency can diverge under power fault; this is a cross-case durability distinction, not an Intel-320-specific failure claim;
158. **interface contract or manufacturer feature claim ≠ empirical implementation compliance** — ATA text and Intel's product disclosure establish specified semantics/design intent, while independent fault injection remains a separate evidence class; anonymized FAST '13 results cannot be reassigned to the SSD 320;
159. **application-visible current state ≠ crash-admissible stable state** — 4.4BSD soft updates lets applications see the newest in-memory metadata while dependency enforcement can write an older or temporarily rolled-back version that remains consistent with the current on-disk image; `current` must be qualified by observer and crash contract;
160. **crash-safe mountability ≠ permanence of the latest operation** — the bounded soft-updates filesystem can be safely mounted immediately after a crash while recent delayed updates disappear; structural integrity and recency durability are different retention targets;
161. **consistency-control metadata ≠ necessarily persistent recovery metadata** — soft-updates dependency structures are primarily in-memory machinery used before a crash to constrain what is allowed onto disk; their effect can be persistent even though the control structures themselves are not a separate durable recovery log;
162. **write ordering can preserve crash invariants without a persistent undo/redo journal** — the bounded soft-updates mechanism uses dependency tracking plus temporary rollback/roll-forward of disk-bound metadata versions; this is one historical regime, not a claim that ordering replaces logging universally;
163. **`fsync` durability ≠ payload-block completion** — in the bounded production FFS implementation, committing a file can require allocation bitmaps, data blocks, indirect blocks, the inode, and unwritten directory/name state; the durable object is relational rather than reducible to its payload sectors;
164. **crash admissibility ≠ leak-free resource accounting** — unclaimed blocks/inodes and conservatively high link counts can remain while the on-disk filesystem is safe for immediate service; later/background reclamation is a separate convergence task;
165. **filesystem durability closure ≠ device flush boundary** — Case 15 grounds a lower ATA volatile-cache → nonvolatile-media relation, while Case 16 grounds higher-level dependency/order closure; a device can durably store individually issued writes without thereby deciding which filesystem combinations are crash-admissible, and filesystem semantics cannot substitute for the device’s own durability contract;
166. **parity redundancy ≠ replica multiplicity** — Ouchi's 1977-filed arrangement reconstructs one missing record segment from surviving segments plus an XOR check sum; no surviving storage unit needs to hold a complete duplicate of the missing contribution;
167. **single-failure service continuity ≠ restored redundancy margin** — Chen et al.'s demand reconstruction can satisfy accesses while a member remains failed, while a separate background process reconstructs the full failed-disk contents; current service and repaired fault tolerance are distinct retention targets;
168. **redundant bytes physically present ≠ usable current redundancy** — Chen et al. show parity becoming inconsistent after interrupted writes and require consistency state/regeneration; a physically readable parity sector can fail the currentness condition required for safe reconstruction;
169. **reconstruction progress can be constitutive retention meta state** — Chen et al. explicitly include how much of a failed disk has been reconstructed among state that must be accurately maintained across crashes; the repair frontier is part of the current array condition rather than merely an operator narrative;
170. **spare capacity ≠ stored payload, while spare capacity can be retention infrastructure** — stand-by spare disks enable demand reconstruction and background restoration even though unused spare space does not yet embody current user data;
171. **RAID reconstruction ≠ RADOS replica repair or HDD sector reassignment** — parity reconstruction can synthesize a missing contribution from a coded group; RADOS repairs from version/currentness-qualified replicas; Case 14's `REASSIGN BLOCKS` changes physical serving location but explicitly does not itself preserve damaged payload. Their similarity is functional at the repair/relocation layer, not historical or mechanistic identity;
172. **RAID nomenclature ≠ invention priority for parity-based disk recovery** — Patterson, Gibson, and Katz ground the 1987–1988 `RAID` name/taxonomy, while Ouchi’s IBM filing from 1977 directly grounds an earlier XOR/check-sum multi-device recovery mechanism; terminology history and mechanism priority must remain separate;
173. **physical presence/readability ≠ verified current integrity** — ZFS's checksum relation can reject a returned block even when underlying I/O yields bytes; material survival and filesystem-level admissibility are separate retention relations;
174. **redundancy availability ≠ defect discovery** — a trustworthy alternate copy can coexist with an unknown damaged copy; until demand access or scrubbing exposes the defect, the system may not know that repair is required;
175. **detection work ≠ repair work** — a scrub performs proactive traversal/verification, while repair is conditional on discovering a problem and having a trustworthy source; a scrub can finish with no repairs, expose an unrepairable fault, or trigger self-healing;
176. **scrub ≠ rebuild/resilver** — scrubbing searches current data for integrity/readability defects not necessarily known in advance, while resilvering reconstructs/copies state already known to need restoration because a device is replaced or out of date;
177. **scheduled maintenance ≠ periodic restoration of every retained state** — unlike DRAM refresh, ZFS scrubbing spends scheduled/proactive work primarily on verification; rewriting is conditional on a detected integrity failure and available redundancy;
178. **nominal redundancy ≠ verified repair margin** — a latent defect can silently consume one path needed for later recovery before the system discovers it; earlier verification can expose and repair the defect while another good embodiment still exists. This is a failure-model-relative engineering conclusion, not a ZFS historical variable;
179. **scrubbing terminology/technique ≠ ZFS invention priority** — Schwarz et al. published a named `disk scrubbing` technique in October 2004, defining periodic all-data access for early failure detection and redundancy-assisted rebuilding before the bounded ZFS documentation used here;
180. **erasure-code algebra ≠ failure-domain independence** — f4’s Reed–Solomon `(10,4)` relation specifies which fragment sets can reconstruct data, while the system separately tries to place data/parity blocks on different racks and uses a placement balancer to correct violations. Coding sufficiency and topological independence are different retention relations;
181. **requested-object read continuity ≠ completed full-fragment repair** — an f4 backoff node can reconstruct only the requested BLOB-sized range after a normal read fails, while a missing full block remains absent until separate offline rebuilder work finishes;
182. **reconstructed content ≠ restored redundancy geometry** — f4 explicitly allows failure/reconstruction/replacement to leave stripe members in the same failure domain and assigns placement balancing as a separate correction step. Recreating bytes and restoring future-failure independence can complete at different times;
183. **distributed erasure coding ≠ replication with smaller copies** — f4 reconstructs missing content from coded relations among companion/parity blocks rather than requiring a surviving complete duplicate, while its small index files remain triple replicated. The two redundancy forms coexist and must not be normalized into one mechanism;
184. **reconstruction can be compositional across failure domains** — f4’s geo-XOR read path can depend on ordinary single-cell backoff reconstruction when an XOR or buddy input is itself unavailable. Higher-level recoverability can therefore depend on lower-level recoverability rather than one flat repair operation;
185. **redundancy policy can be state-class-specific inside one system** — f4 Reed–Solomon-encodes bulk BLOB data, triple-replicates index files, uses separate metadata/name-node protection, and adds geo-XOR for datacenter failure. `The system is erasure-coded` is too coarse to identify how every constitutive state remains;
186. **coded-fragment survival ≠ service readability or physical erasure** — f4’s bounded key-delete path makes an encrypted BLOB unreadable by deleting its externally stored key without requiring immediate compaction of the coded data. This supports relation-level logical forgetting, not a claim that physical fragments are erased or universally unrecoverable;
187. **production erasure-coded system design ≠ erasure-code invention priority** — f4’s own Related Work says it uses erasure codes as tools and does not innovate in this area, while Reed and Solomon’s canonical polynomial-code paper dates to 1960. The historical contribution grounded here is the 2014 system organization, placement, and repair semantics, not coding-theory priority;
188. **command completion ≠ nonvolatile-media persistence unless the applicable contract establishes it** — NVMe 1.0 defines generic completion separately from VWC, Flush, and Write FUA; FUA is the explicit per-write clause that requires nonvolatile-media residency before that command completes, so generic completion must not be silently upgraded into a stronger persistence promise;
189. **per-command persistence ≠ cross-command ordering** — NVMe 1.0 Write FUA requires the addressed data to reach nonvolatile media before completion and immediately states that there is no implied ordering with other commands; application-required ordering remains a separate host/software relation;
190. **Flush ≠ FUA** — Revision 1.0 defines Flush as moving data in volatile storage toward nonvolatile memory, while FUA attaches a media-before-completion guarantee to one Write. They can participate in the same durability strategy without being the same control operation or scope;
191. **interface volatility class ≠ simple physical-substrate class** — the VWC feature says a write cache guaranteed to drain to nonvolatile media on power loss is considered nonvolatile for that feature. The interface classification therefore depends on a recovery guarantee and does not by itself identify the cache-cell technology;
192. **normal-operation atomicity ≠ power-fail atomicity** — AWUN and AWUPF are separately reported and §6.3 allows the normal atomic write unit to differ from the power-fail unit; ordinary-power atomicity cannot be assumed to define the failure-transition regime;
193. **post-write currentness ≠ persistence order/history** — Revision 1.0 requires later-completing reads of a written location to return the completed write's data while separately assigning general inter-command ordering to host/application software; visibility/currentness of one LBA and durable ordering among several operations are different relations;
194. **standard persistence semantics ≠ product PLP mechanism or invention priority** — Case 20 grounds a 2011 interface contract, not capacitors, firmware, or a named-controller fault result. Case 15 already grounds earlier 2007 ATA Flush semantics, so no `NVMe invented Flush/FUA` story follows from the bounded source;
195. **environmental control can be constitutive retention infrastructure without being the retained payload** — the delay-line sources directly couple mercury transit time, temperature, timing/phase, oscillator correction, and heater control. Temperature-control state and heater work do not encode the user's word, yet failure of that relation can make the circulating pulse positions unusable. This is an engineering reconstruction grounded in period mechanisms, not a universal claim that every storage medium requires active climate control;
196. **refresh obligation ≠ recurring command-generation responsibility** — Micron's 1999 SDRAM retains the same deadline-driven need for row restoration while normal AUTO REFRESH requires a new external command each time and SELF REFRESH lets the device generate its own recurring refresh work after mode entry;
197. **internal refresh addressing ≠ autonomous recurrence** — Micron calls AUTO REFRESH nonpersistent even though the internal refresh controller generates row addressing. `Who names the row` and `who causes the next maintenance event` remain distinct relations at the SDRAM interface;
198. **retention availability ≠ ordinary service availability** — in SELF REFRESH all SDRAM inputs other than CKE become `Don't Care`; the payload can remain actively retained while ordinary READ/WRITE service is suspended until the documented exit sequence completes;
199. **maintenance responsibility can be mode-transferred and reversible** — CKE-controlled SELF REFRESH entry shifts recurring clocking/refresh work into the SDRAM, while exit through stable CLK/CKE/`tXSR` returns the device to a regime requiring externally issued AUTO REFRESH commands. `Responsibility` is project reconstruction vocabulary, not Micron's period term;
200. **self-refresh autonomy ≠ nonvolatility** — Micron says the device retains data without external clocking and can do so while the rest of the system is powered down, but it also says the SDRAM provides internal clocking and performs its own refresh cycles. The bounded source does not establish unpowered retention of the SDRAM itself;
201. **mode-transition timing can be constitutive service-recovery infrastructure** — self-refresh exit requires stable CLK and NOPs through `tXSR` because an internal refresh may still be in progress; retained payload and readiness for ordinary service can therefore have different completion times;
202. **manufacturer command semantics ≠ invention priority or complete standard history** — Micron Rev. 11/99 grounds one named product family's interface behavior. Earlier TI/Toshiba cases already block a novelty story for internal refresh/autonomous scheduling, and a JEDEC chronology requires separate normative sources.
203. **virtual-page identity ≠ page-frame identity ≠ backing-slot identity** — IBM's period definitions distinguish virtual pages from real page frames, while the 1976 ASM allocates auxiliary slots dynamically when page-out is required; one page's operational identity therefore need not be tied to one permanent real or external embodiment;
204. **residency ≠ currentness** — a page can be resident yet either unchanged relative to an existing external copy or changed such that the real copy contains newer information that must be preserved before frame reuse;
205. **frame reassignment ≠ forgetting** — IBM explicitly allows an unchanged page's real frame to be made available without another page-out when a usable external copy already exists; ending one residency relation need not end the virtual page's recoverability;
206. **page replacement ≠ unconditional page-out** — the documented change-state relation determines whether preservation work is required before a selected real frame can safely be reused; capacity pressure creates a conditional, not universal, writeback obligation;
207. **nonresidency ≠ loss or immediate availability** — a page marked out of real storage can remain recoverable through page fault and page-in, while ordinary service still waits on successful recovery work;
208. **working backing storage ≠ crash-durable application retention** — IBM's external page storage supports the virtual-storage hierarchy, but the bounded sources do not establish an application durability promise across arbitrary crash, reboot, or administrative reuse;
209. **paging relocation ≠ cache eviction, FTL relocation, or defect remapping** — all can preserve a higher-level designation across changed embodiment, but OS/VS2 frame reuse is capacity/residency-triggered and conditionally propagates changed state; the historical triggers, authority, and mechanisms remain distinct.
210. **replica multiplicity ≠ one already-selected current value** — Dynamo can retain and return several causally unrelated leaf versions for one key; in this bounded regime the admissible current state can remain plural until reconciliation, unlike a model in which one replica/value is already authoritative.
211. **causal ancestry can authorize forgetting while causal incomparability can create a retention obligation** — Dynamo's vector-clock comparison lets an ancestor version be forgotten, but concurrent branches must remain until reconciliation establishes a successor; existence of a newer-looking value alone does not prove safe discard.
212. **write availability ≠ intended-placement convergence** — sloppy quorum can accept a write on a reachable substitute node while a hint retains the intended recipient and later handoff remains outstanding; successful service and steady-state replica placement can complete at different times.
213. **hinted payload ≠ complete repair relation** — the temporary replica bytes do not by themselves identify why they are temporary or where they should return; the retained hint is control metadata constitutive of the future handoff obligation.
214. **replica-divergence detection ≠ synchronization completion** — Merkle-tree comparison can localize keys that are out of sync before the corresponding payload synchronization action completes; proof of difference and repair of difference are separate retention work.
215. **read completion ≠ replica convergence** — Dynamo can return a read after its response threshold while the coordinator continues waiting briefly for stale replies and performs read repair afterward; foreground availability can precede repair completion.
216. **anti-entropy ≠ integrity scrubbing or physical refresh** — Dynamo's background anti-entropy compares replica currentness and synchronizes divergent distributed copies; ZFS scrub verifies checksum-qualified media/object integrity and DRAM refresh restores decaying physical state. Their shared maintenance shape is functional analogy, not one historical mechanism.
217. **Dynamo divergent-version currentness ≠ RADOS peering currentness** — both retain version/repair metadata, but the bounded RADOS case establishes a correct placement-group state before normal I/O while Dynamo can expose several causally unrelated versions for application reconciliation; distributed `currentness` is therefore itself regime-specific.
218. **background retention maintenance can be explicitly resource-budgeted against foreground service** — Dynamo's production admission controller limits replica-synchronization and handoff work when it harms put/get latency; automated repair is real work whose completion time can be shaped by service policy rather than disappearing behind the interface.
219. **coded recoverability ≠ reconstruction cost** — WAS compares `RS (12,4)` and `LRC (12,2,2)` at the same `1.33x` storage cost while the common single-data-fragment reconstruction reads twelve versus six fragments respectively. Knowing that enough information survives does not specify how much distributed state must be mobilized to recover it;
220. **local reconstruction ≠ physical co-location** — LRC locality is a smaller code-dependency/read set, while WAS separately distributes fragments across rack fault domains and arranges upgrade domains; corresponding members can share one planned-offline domain while remaining in different hardware failure domains;
221. **on-demand reconstruction availability ≠ durable fragment repair** — a reconstruction read can decode, cache, and return a requested fragment, whereas extended EN/disk unavailability causes the Stream Manager to reconstruct onto another EN and write the result to disk;
222. **coded-fragment presence ≠ completed redundancy-regime handoff** — asynchronous conversion can leave new fragments and persisted progress while the full replicas still remain; decode/CRC validation, coded-fragment persistence, extent completion metadata, and only then source-replica deletion define a stronger completion relation;
223. **transition progress/completion metadata can be constitutive retention state** — progress persisted into new fragments lets another EN resume interrupted coding, while fragment boundaries and completion flags participate in deciding that the coded representation is ready to replace the previous full replicas;
224. **representation maintenance can be off the foreground write path without being optional work** — active writes are acknowledged through the replica set; sealed extents are coded asynchronously according to policy/load, and system throttling must budget coding/reconstruction against foreground service and critical re-replication;
225. **repair locality is a cost/fault-tolerance design coordinate, not free durability** — the bounded LRC is explicitly non-MDS and uses local parity to lower common reconstruction cost while failure-pattern recoverability remains code-specific; lower read-set cost cannot be promoted into a universal stronger-durability claim;
226. **fault domain ≠ upgrade domain** — WAS distinguishes correlated hardware failure from nodes intentionally taken offline together during rolling upgrades. Planned maintenance topology can therefore constrain retention availability even when no storage medium has physically failed;
227. **2012 WAS LRC production use ≠ invention of erasure coding or the storage/repair trade-off** — the paper itself situates LRC among prior codes and calls it an improvement over the authors’ Pyramid Codes; Microsoft’s 2007 Pyramid record already frames a storage-space/access-efficiency trade-off, while Case 19 separately retains the older Reed–Solomon boundary.
228. **fragment presence ≠ committed object version** — Swift 2.3.0 can leave timestamped EC `.data` archives after a proxy dies before commit while the client receives no success and the fragments remain stale cleanup candidates; physically surviving newer coded bytes need not constitute retained service state;
229. **coded recoverability ≠ version admissibility** — Swift 2.10.1 GET requires enough distinct fragment indexes at the same timestamp plus a same-timestamp durability indication. Erasure-code algebra determines a decodable symbol set, while the object protocol separately determines which version-coherent set may answer;
230. **newer timestamp ≠ safe-forgetting authority** — both bounded Swift source states delay deletion of older timestamp files until the replacement has crossed the commit phase; temporal/version ordering alone is weaker than demonstrated replacement retention;
231. **fragment-count sufficiency ≠ version-coherent cohort sufficiency** — the proxy may encounter several timestamps and duplicate fragment indexes, so `enough files` is not the same condition as `enough complementary fragments belonging to one retained object version`;
232. **durability/currentness metadata can qualify a distributed cohort rather than one payload fragment** — in 2.10.1 one same-timestamp `.durable` indication can qualify fragment archives returned from other nodes that lack a local marker, making the commit fact relational and set-level;
233. **client success ≠ complete coded-placement convergence** — after the documented PUT success threshold, the reconstructor can still restore missing fragment indexes, revert handoffs, and propagate durability markers; service completion and repaired steady state need not coincide;
234. **mutable EC currentness ≠ Dynamo divergent currentness or WAS immutable representation handoff** — Dynamo can expose causally unrelated leaves for application reconciliation, WAS Case 24 converts sealed immutable extents from replicas to coded state, while Swift Case 25 selects one same-timestamp committed coded cohort during mutable object replacement. The comparison is functional, not one shared historical mechanism;
235. **implementation release ≠ timeless durability semantics, and fragment existence ≠ fragment validity** — Swift 2.3.0 and 2.10.1 document different commit thresholds, while the 2.10.1 release also adds fragment-size/metadata validation and quarantine after a concrete ssync defect. Protocol thresholds and implementation validity checks must be cited to the source state rather than universalized.
236. **replica multiplicity ≠ valid replica count** — GFS explicitly warns that an inactive corrupted replica can make the master believe a chunk has enough valid replicas until the defect is discovered; redundancy quantity must therefore be qualified by replica usability.
237. **version currentness ≠ integrity validity** — chunk version numbers exclude stale replicas while per-block checksums detect corruption of stored data. A copy can fail the currentness relation or the integrity relation, and one mechanism does not substitute for the other.
238. **replica divergence ≠ corruption** — GFS states that divergent replicas may be legal under its mutation semantics, especially record append, so bytewise replica equality cannot serve as the universal integrity authority in this bounded system; local checksum relations do that work.
239. **demand-time verification ≠ proactive idle-time discovery** — normal reads verify requested checksum blocks, while idle chunkservers can scan inactive chunks specifically to reveal corruption in rarely read state before ordinary demand happens to expose it.
240. **defect discovery ≠ fallback service ≠ restored redundancy margin** — after a checksum failure the requester can use another replica while the master separately clones a valid replacement; good service can resume before the replication goal has been durably restored.
241. **checksum metadata ≠ payload, but can be constitutive retention state** — GFS stores per-block checksums separately from user data because later verification and repair-source qualification depend on the data/checksum relation.
242. **background integrity repair has a resource budget** — clone concurrency and bandwidth are explicitly limited so re-replication does not overwhelm clients, while inactive-chunk verification is scheduled during idle periods; background maintenance is real competed-for work.
243. **GFS idle verification ≠ ZFS `scrub` ≠ Dynamo anti-entropy** — the 2003 GFS paper documents `scan and verify` of inactive chunks, functionally predating this repository's 2004 `disk scrubbing` terminology anchor without using that word; ZFS later names pool scrubbing, while Dynamo anti-entropy targets replica divergence/currentness rather than local-copy corruption. This is a functional/prior-art boundary, not one shared historical mechanism or an invention claim.
244. **coded recoverability ≠ integrity-qualified recoverability** — Luminous EC profiles describe which chunk-loss patterns remain decodable, but BlueStore checksum/deep-scrub support exists because surviving indexed chunks still need local integrity qualification before nominal coding margin can be treated as trustworthy.
245. **checksum presence ≠ checksum authority** — the 12.2.6 regression left some stored per-object checksum values stale; 12.2.7 therefore added a mode that explicitly distrusts those digests. Retaining a verifier is weaker than retaining a verifier the system may safely believe.
246. **checksum mismatch ≠ universal proof of payload corruption** — the Luminous regression provides a concrete counterexample in which the maintained checksum relation itself was inconsistent and could produce `EIO`; a mismatch establishes a diagnostic inconsistency, not automatically which side of the relation is wrong.
247. **integrity metadata is maintained retained state** — BlueStore checksum values consume metadata space, are updated through writes, read during verification, and in the bounded incident had to be repaired. They are constitutive operational state rather than timeless annotations outside the storage system.
248. **checksum existence ≠ scrub coverage** — Luminous separately schedules and throttles scrub/deep-scrub work. Stored checksums may exist long before a background pass has exercised the relevant population, so verification coverage has its own temporality and resource budget.
249. **nominal EC margin ≠ verified repair confidence** — `K/M` geometry and CRUSH placement can remain nominally intact while hidden corruption or untrusted digest state reduces confidence in which surviving chunks are safe future reconstruction inputs.
250. **full verification coverage can be a repair milestone** — the 12.2.8 procedure required a full-cluster deep scrub and resolution of digest inconsistencies before the temporary distrust workaround could be disabled; returning verifier authority was gated by completed verification/repair work.
251. **mutable EC support can depend on lower-layer integrity semantics** — Luminous permits `allow_ec_overwrites` only on BlueStore because its checksumming is used for deep-scrub corruption detection. A high-level coding feature can therefore depend on a lower storage layer retaining and maintaining integrity state.
252. **Ceph Luminous deep scrub ≠ GFS `scan and verify` ≠ ZFS scrub, and later Ceph ≠ early RADOS** — these regimes are functionally comparable at proactive integrity maintenance, but their historical vocabulary, redundancy form, currentness rules, verifier state, and release semantics differ. Shared function or project lineage is not one historical mechanism.

253. **deletion ≠ immediate absence of retained state** — Swift 2.10.1 materializes DELETE as a timestamped `.ts` tombstone so the deletion itself can remain the newest synchronizable object state while older positive embodiments may still exist elsewhere.
254. **local absence ≠ distributed proof of absence** — a replica can be isolated while DELETE occurs; removing local payload does not by itself communicate versioned negative currentness to that stale copy, while the tombstone can be propagated and ordered against it.
255. **payload retirement ≠ tombstone retirement** — older `.data`, `.meta`, `.durable`, or fragment state can cease to be admissible before the tombstone is itself old enough for reclamation; positive-state forgetting and delete-marker forgetting are distinct events.
256. **negative state can be constitutive retention state** — the tombstone is not application payload, yet its timestamp/kind relation decides that older payload must not answer as current. Technical retention can therefore preserve non-admissibility rather than positive content.
257. **reclamation age ≠ physical retention lifetime** — Swift's `reclaim_age` is a configurable protocol/maintenance interval tied by the release documentation to the consistency window and replica convergence, not a medium-decay constant or a secure-erasure deadline.
258. **forgetting can require temporary remembrance** — in this bounded regime, making an object remain forgotten across asynchronous replicas requires retaining the fact of deletion for a while. This is an engineering reconstruction from the tombstone mechanism, not a universal philosophical identity between memory and forgetting.
259. **tombstone precedence extends across replicated and EC on-disk state** — release-matched unit tests make a newer or equal-timestamp tombstone defeat older positive `.data`/`.meta` state and, in EC cases, prior `.durable`/fragment state. Negative currentness is versioned control logic rather than empty disk space.
260. **Swift tombstone reclamation ≠ Flash garbage collection, cache eviction, or Dynamo reconciliation** — each can retire an embodiment or alter currentness, but Swift's mechanism is a timestamped negative object version propagated across replicas and later reclaimed. The comparison is functional, not historical or mechanistic identity.
261. **delete-marker retention has a correctness/capacity trade-off** — keeping tombstones longer spends storage, scans, and synchronization work on anti-resurrection evidence; reclaiming sooner releases that control state while narrowing the failure/convergence interval it can cover. The bounded sources justify the trade-off relation, not one universal safe duration.

262. **scrub-authoritative candidate set ≠ EC minimum decode set** — Luminous `v12.2.8` first constructs repair candidates from scrub/ObjectInfo/error evidence, then the EC backend separately asks `minimum_to_decode` for a coding-sufficient subset of currently usable shard indexes. Operational admissibility and algebraic sufficiency are different retained relations.
263. **operational repair authority ≠ certainty of correct payload** — the implementation itself warns that an auth shard can reach the repair-candidate path without the system knowing that it has the "correct" data. `authoritative` is therefore a procedural role under available evidence, not proof of objective truth.
264. **primary preference ≠ unconditional source authority** — `be_select_auth_object()` scans the primary first so it wins otherwise-equal ties, but recorded shard errors disqualify it and higher object versions outrank it. Physical/topological role and repair-source admissibility remain distinct.
265. **integrity diagnosis ≠ recovery-state transition** — scrub comparison identifies missing/inconsistent peers; `repair_object()` then mutates `peer_missing`/local missing and `missing_loc` so ordinary recovery treats the diagnosed bad shard as reconstructable missing state. Detection alone does not schedule the same work as missing-state injection.
266. **physical shard presence ≠ decode-source availability** — EC recovery excludes shards marked missing or newly reported in `error_shards`; a physically present shard can therefore cease to count as a possible decode input without first disappearing from the medium.
267. **planned decode source set ≠ stable source set** — read failures can add new `error_shards`, force recomputation of available indexes, and invoke `minimum_to_decode` again. Retained repair-source relations can change during the repair attempt itself.
268. **repair bookkeeping ≠ reconstructed redundancy** — calling `repair_object()` and recording missing/source locations precede successful EC reads, `ECUtil::decode`, and installation. A PG can know what must be repaired before the missing coded contribution has actually been restored.
269. **negative repair state is constitutive of coded retention** — `missing`, inconsistent/error flags, and excluded-source information are not payload, yet they prevent bad embodiments from participating in reconstruction and guide the system toward a surviving admissible decode set.
270. **Ceph Luminous scrub repair ≠ GFS replica cloning ≠ Swift EC cohort admissibility** — all qualify distributed repair state, but GFS selects whole valid replicas, Swift selects a committed same-version coded cohort, and Luminous here converts scrub evidence into candidate authority/missing state before codec-level minimum decoding. The comparison is functional, not one protocol genealogy.

271. **persistent interface contract ≠ fixed physical substrate** — NVMe 1.4 PMR explicitly permits the persistence guarantee to be realized either by completed nonvolatile-memory placement or by a nonvolatile write buffer that is transferred to nonvolatile memory later. The host-visible guarantee underdetermines the internal embodiment.
272. **Posted-write issue/completion ≠ persistence-barrier completion** — `PMRCAP.PMRWBM` exposes read-based mechanisms whose completion establishes that earlier Posted PCIe writes to PMR have completed and are persistent. Issuing ordinary memory writes and establishing their persistence are different interface relations.
273. **persistent write ≠ final internal placement** — the PMR contract can be satisfied by nonvolatile buffered state before a later transfer to nonvolatile memory, so persistence at the interface does not imply that the bytes already occupy their final internal location.
274. **PMR ready ≠ prior contents restored correctly** — `PMRSTS.HSTS = Restore Error` permits a PMR that is operating and persistent while its contents may not match those from before the preceding power/reset/disable transition. Persistence capability and state continuity are separate.
275. **successful request completion ≠ valid recovery or mutation** — when PMR is not ready, reads may complete successfully with undefined values and writes may complete normally without updating memory. Completion must be qualified by interface state.
276. **persistence ≠ immediate availability** — contents may persist across disable/reset while saving/restoring takes time and `NRDY` gates ordinary PCIe memory service. Surviving state and currently callable state are different relations.
277. **persistent capability ≠ perpetual integrity/health authority** — `HSTS` and `ERR` can qualify reads, writes, and prior persistence after the region has been established; delayed health reporting or polling can leave an interval whose operations must be treated as potentially affected.
278. **NVMe PMR persistence ≠ NVMe namespace Flush/FUA semantics** — Case 20 uses queued namespace/LBA commands, VWC, Flush, FUA, and command completion; Case 30 uses memory-mapped PMR, Posted PCIe writes, read-based persistence barriers, and ready/restore/health state. The relation is functional, not a timeless single protocol.
279. **ordinary PMR disable ≠ sanitize-induced unrecoverability** — PMR disable is explicitly inside the persistence guarantee, while the not-ready behavior following sanitize must prevent recovery of previous user data from cache or nonvolatile media. Logical unavailability, retained disable-state continuity, and deliberate forgetting remain distinct.

280. **persistence domain ≠ persistent medium** — SNIA Version 1 defines a software-visible location/boundary for durability across restart while deliberately spanning multiple NVM device models; the term does not name one universal chip, controller, or media technology.
281. **persistence-domain arrival ≠ unconditional recoverability** — §6.9 says data that reached the domain may be recoverable and conditions recovery on whether the actual failure pattern is tolerated by the domain's design/configuration; survival is qualified by a failure envelope.
282. **store execution ≠ persistence qualification** — mapped PM writes may remain in processor-resident caches or memory-controller buffers before reaching a persistence domain, so direct load/store access does not eliminate a durability boundary.
283. **successful synchronization ≠ write atomicity** — `NVM.PM.FILE.SYNC` guarantees the requested range reaches the persistence domain by successful completion while explicitly withholding write atomicity; durable arrival and failure-consistent update are separate properties.
284. **persistence synchronization ≠ ordering guarantee** — `NVM.PM.FILE.OPTIMIZED_FLUSH` explicitly supplies neither atomicity nor ordering for synchronized byte ranges; a higher-level consistency protocol cannot be inferred from the durability primitive.
285. **sync completion ≠ exact persistence timestamp** — SNIA allows a range to reach the persistence domain before the sync action, so successful completion closes an obligation by a deadline rather than proving when every byte crossed the boundary.
286. **multiple persistence domains ≠ one global machine durability boundary** — SNIA allows several domains in one system and makes their alignment with volumes/filesystems an administrative act, so durability configuration can participate in the retained recovery relation.
287. **interrupted flush progress ≠ retained completion map** — failure during optimized flush can leave some ranges persistent and others not, with no indication of which ranges completed; physical progress and knowledge of that progress are distinct states.
288. **historical term ownership ≠ functional similarity** — `persistence domain` is directly documented in SNIA's approved 2013 programming model, which separately cites NVMe 1.1; inspected ratified NVMe 1.4 and 2.0 use `Persistent Memory Region`/PMR barrier vocabulary and contain no exact-text match for the phrase. This supports a bounded vocabulary distinction, not a universal absence or invention claim.
289. **SNIA persistence domain ≠ NVMe PMR** — both can participate in software claims that earlier writes are persistent, but SNIA's term denotes a cross-layer durability location/failure boundary while PMR is a named NVMe PCIe memory region with its own barrier/readiness/health semantics; the comparison is functional, not synonymy or proven genealogy.

290. **persistence-domain membership ≠ physical nonvolatile-media residency** — Intel ADR places memory-controller WPQ state inside a power-fail-safe persistence domain because the platform guarantees a failure-triggered drain, even though the bytes may not yet reside in the persistent-memory DIMM.
291. **ADR-protected WPQ ≠ processor-cache persistence** — Intel explicitly keeps processor caches outside the ADR-only protected path; software must still move relevant modified cache lines toward the memory subsystem before relying on ADR.
292. **eADR domain expansion ≠ elimination of ordering/fencing** — eADR extends power-fail protection into processor caches and can remove ordinary PMDK cache-flush operations, while Intel still requires `SFENCE`; enlarging the protected physical path does not create every persistence ordering relation.
293. **same persistent-memory medium ≠ same software persistence obligation** — ADR and eADR can terminate in the same broad class of persistent memory while the required cache-flush work differs according to the platform capability exposed to software.
294. **power-fail protection ≠ universal crash/reset/failure survivability** — the Intel sources ground power-failure behavior and, for ADR, shutdown draining; they do not establish arbitrary reset, firmware-fault, media-corruption, transaction, or software-crash semantics.
295. **persistent qualification can depend on guaranteed future transfer work** — ADR/eADR can classify volatile intermediate state as protected because power-fail signaling, drain logic, and available energy promise a later transfer into persistent memory before the failure completes.
296. **stored energy ≠ payload, while stored energy can be retention infrastructure** — Intel requires additional OEM stored energy for eADR; the reserve carries no user bytes but is part of the mechanism that makes failure-triggered cache draining credible.
297. **platform capability detection can change which retention work software performs** — Intel states that PMDK detects eADR and can omit explicit flush operations when it is present, making the persistence algorithm conditional on discoverable platform state rather than only on the memory module.
298. **ADR/eADR ≠ NVMe Flush/FUA/PMR or SSD-controller PLP** — the bounded similarity is power-fail persistence work; the historical interfaces, protected state, authority, and transfer paths differ across Cases 15, 20, 30, and 32.
299. **ADR `Asynchronous DRAM Refresh` ≠ SDRAM `SELF REFRESH`** — Intel's ADR source describes imminent-power-fail signaling and WPQ draining, while Case 21 grounds recurring in-device DRAM refresh under a retention mode; shared `refresh` vocabulary does not establish mechanism identity.

300. **refresh obligation ≠ service-blocking scope** — Micron DDR5 `Same Bank Refresh` keeps periodic maintenance while narrowing which banked resources are unavailable together; the need to refresh does not determine the global scope of lost service.
301. **same-bank refresh ≠ one globally singular bank** — Micron's more precise public wording is `one bank per bank group` / `a bank in each BG`; the historical name must not be flattened into a topology the manufacturer does not describe.
302. **maintenance localization ≠ maintenance elimination** — `REFsb` is presented as an improved refresh scheme whose performance/availability benefit comes from narrower interference, not from making dynamic cells quiescent or nonvolatile.
303. **non-target-bank availability ≠ refreshed-bank availability** — the 2023 Micron platform description explicitly keeps other bank groups accessible while refresh occurs, so `memory available during refresh` is target-relative rather than one Boolean device property.
304. **refresh target geometry ≠ refresh schedule authority** — Case 21's normal/self-refresh handoff concerns who generates recurring maintenance work; Case 33 concerns which bank subset a refresh operation targets. Neither dimension implies the other.
305. **bank-group concurrency ≠ a new retention substrate** — DDR5 can expose more useful ordinary-access concurrency during maintenance while retaining the same broad class of volatile dynamic state whose preservation still depends on refresh.
306. **DDR5 `Same Bank Refresh` ≠ SDRAM `SELF REFRESH`** — Case 21 internalizes recurring clocking/refresh during a CKE-controlled retention mode; Case 33 localizes maintenance interference across bank groups. Shared `refresh` vocabulary does not establish mechanism identity.
307. **DDR5 `Same Bank Refresh` ≠ generic historical `per-bank refresh`** — Micron's own DDR5 comparison preserves `same bank` as the refresh name, while `per bank` appears separately in command taxonomy; LPDDR or other per-bank-refresh regimes require their own period evidence.
308. **manufacturer feature semantics ≠ complete normative timing contract** — public Micron HTML is sufficient to ground the bounded target/availability relation, but exact `REFsb` timing, sequencing, fine-granularity restrictions, and controller compliance remain open until a directly inspected normative specification or product datasheet supports them.

309. **retention constraint ≠ one universal fixed refresh interval** — Micron's bounded design exists because a worst-case high-temperature cadence can be conservative at lower temperature; the selected interval is a policy for an environmental envelope, not evidence of a temperature-independent physical loss deadline.
310. **retention obligation ≠ worst-case maintenance frequency** — lower-temperature cadence reduction saves refresh work while leaving dynamic-cell preservation dependent on recurring restoration.
311. **temperature observation ≠ direct payload-retention measurement** — the nearby sensor is documented as reflecting DRAM temperature; it does not read user bits or directly measure every cell/row's remaining retention time.
312. **adaptive refresh ≠ zero guardband** — Micron explicitly uses discrete threshold steps as guardbanding; environment adaptation replaces one global worst-case policy with another bounded sensing/classification policy rather than abolishing margin.
313. **temperature-band granularity ≠ cell-retention granularity** — the five-band embodiment is a control discretization, and the patent itself permits more/fewer bands; it is not evidence that DRAM cells possess five intrinsic retention states.
314. **maintenance optimization ≠ zero-cost retention infrastructure** — increasing comparator/band resolution can consume enough power to exceed the refresh-power savings, so sensing/control work is part of the retention-energy budget.
315. **temperature-conditioned cadence ≠ self-refresh authority** — Micron's 1991 circuit feeds system logic that produces refresh, while later patents explicitly combine temperature sensing with self-refresh; the condition that changes cadence and the locus of recurring maintenance authority are separable.
316. **temperature-conditioned array refresh ≠ per-row retention-aware scheduling** — Case 34 changes an array-level cadence from an environmental proxy; it does not profile individual rows or assign row-specific refresh periods.
317. **Micron 1991 temperature-dependent refresh ≠ invention priority for temperature-adaptive DRAM refresh** — US5278796A itself cites U.S. Patent 4,920,489, whose 1987-priority family already describes thermistor/A-D/table-controlled ambient-temperature refresh.
318. **historical temperature/refresh rule of thumb ≠ timeless semiconductor law** — the Micron patent explicitly says its bands and refresh rates may change with technology, so its illustrative temperature multiplier and 8/16/32/64/128 ms schedule must remain period-bounded.

319. **register-field presence ≠ effective software authority** — the bounded Mobile DDR EMR still displays TCSR-labelled positions while the datasheet explicitly says programming them has no effect because the on-die sensor controls the self-refresh oscillator.
320. **temperature-conditioned cadence ≠ host-visible cadence programmability** — automatic on-die sensing can select a temperature-appropriate self-refresh rate without granting the controller effective TCSR-bit authority on this product version.
321. **external-clock absence ≠ maintenance absence** — SELF REFRESH retains array data without external clocking precisely because the SDRAM supplies internal clocking and performs its own recurring refresh cycles.
322. **self-refresh cadence authority ≠ retained-array coverage authority** — the device automatically controls cadence from temperature while PASR separately lets the controller choose which banks/segments continue receiving refresh.
323. **self-refresh active ≠ whole-array retained** — under PASR, only selected regions are refreshed and the datasheet explicitly warns that data in excluded regions will be lost.
324. **array-payload retention ≠ mode/control-state retention** — Deep Power-Down stops retaining array data while the documented mode-register and extended-mode-register values survive exit from DPD; the physical mechanism for that control-state survival remains unspecified.
325. **lower retention cost ≠ one uniform reduction of retention work** — TCSR reduces maintenance frequency, PASR reduces maintained state coverage, and DPD abandons array retention; three power-saving features change different retention relations.
326. **automatic commercial TCSR ≠ invention priority or complete JEDEC genealogy** — Case 34 already grounds earlier temperature-adaptive prior art, while this product datasheet and Micron technical note do not replace revision-by-revision normative JEDEC evidence.
327. **low-power mode ≠ retention mode** — ordinary power-down performs no refresh and is time-limited by the refresh period, SELF REFRESH performs internal maintenance, and DPD intentionally loses array payload; low-power states require mode-specific retention semantics.

328. **nonvolatile medium ≠ maintenance-free reliable retention at a specified error target** — the 2012 FCR proposal starts from NAND cells that remain nonvolatile yet accumulate time/wear-dependent retention errors; periodic controller work is proposed to keep those errors inside an ECC-qualified reliability margin without redefining Flash as DRAM-like volatile memory.
329. **raw physical error accumulation ≠ immediate logical payload loss** — the controller can ECC-correct a page whose raw NAND read already contains retention errors, so physical degradation can precede host-visible logical failure.
330. **ECC-correctability margin ≠ indefinite retention margin** — correctable errors are surviving error budget, not proof that maintenance can be postponed without bound; FCR is deliberately scheduled before accumulated errors exceed the chosen ECC capability.
331. **long logical retention interval ≠ one uninterrupted physical-embodiment interval** — the paper’s bounded model uses repeated short refresh intervals to satisfy a longer storage objective; the specific three-day/three-year numbers are experiment/model results, not universal NAND constants.
332. **Flash FCR `refresh` ≠ DRAM refresh** — FCR may remap corrected data to a newly erased block or add charge in place through ISPP, while DRAM refresh restores volatile dynamic-cell state through a different substrate, granularity, and control path. Shared vocabulary supports a functional comparison, not mechanism identity.
333. **retention maintenance ≠ location stability** — remapping-based FCR preserves a logical designation by programming corrected data to a new physical block and updating the map, extending Case 04’s identity/location separation under a new trigger: retention-error margin rather than ordinary rewrite/reclamation pressure.
334. **more maintenance ≠ more lifetime** — more frequent remapping adds erase/reclaim cycles, and the paper reports workload regimes in which increasing refresh frequency eventually reduces projected lifetime.
335. **maintenance operation ≠ error-neutral repair** — in-place reprogramming can repair charge-loss retention errors while also introducing program-interference errors and cannot remove charge to repair the opposite error direction; hybrid FCR therefore needs a distinct remap fallback.
336. **refresh cadence ≠ one fixed medium constant** — adaptive-rate FCR begins with no refresh in the bounded early-life regime and increases cadence with P/E wear and observed error behavior; maintenance frequency is policy/state dependent rather than one timeless property of NAND.
337. **payload retention can depend on retained maintenance metadata** — adaptive FCR relies on per-block P/E-cycle and validity state already maintained for wear leveling; those controller records are not user payload but help determine future preservation work.
338. **FCR proposal/evaluation ≠ commercial deployment** — the 46× headline is produced by SSD simulation driven by measured chip characterization and workload traces; it must not be rewritten as a measured multi-year production-drive lifetime or universal deployed controller contract.
339. **retention refresh ≠ integrity scrub** — ZFS/GFS verification cases seek evidence that retained copies are already corrupt/inconsistent, whereas FCR is a proactive controller policy intended to renew an aging NAND page before time/wear-dependent raw errors outrun ECC. Both can run in background, but their failure models and repair semantics differ.

340. **logical payload continuity ≠ retrieval-performance continuity** — Samsung framed the bounded 840 EVO incident as old-data read-performance degradation rather than data loss; a value can remain recoverable while the throughput/latency cost of retrieving it degrades sharply.
341. **cell-state survival ≠ constant-cost interpretation of cell state** — Samsung's 2014 explanation says SSD software calibrates changes in cell status over time and that the faulty 840 EVO algorithm performed read-retry aggressively; interpreting a surviving physical state can itself acquire age-dependent work.
342. **successful read-retry ≠ bounded read-service cost** — repeated recovery attempts can still return the intended logical bytes while violating the expected performance envelope; recoverability and efficient service are separate retention targets.
343. **read-path improvement ≠ rewrite-based renewal** — PC Perspective measured a large stale-data improvement immediately after the 2015 firmware update, before the drive had time for background refresh; the reviewer's exact read-algorithm explanation remains inference, but the timing rejects `all improvement = later rewriting`.
344. **one successful restoration ≠ stable future maintenance closure** — Samsung's 2014 rewrite-based Performance Restoration restored old-data speed, yet stale samples later slowed again and the 2015 remediation introduced a firmware policy described as periodic refresh.
345. **unpowered physical persistence ≠ unpowered availability of maintenance work** — Samsung explicitly said the periodic-refresh algorithm does not operate while power is off; NAND can retain payload across an unpowered interval while the controller loses the opportunity to perform performance-maintenance work.
346. **power-off retention interval ≠ powered maintenance opportunity** — Samsung separately identified insufficient run-time and extended power-off as circumstances in which Advanced Performance Optimization might be needed, making available powered time part of the service-maintenance relation without redefining NAND as volatile.
347. **automatic background maintenance ≠ zero service cost** — Samsung acknowledged possible occasional foreground performance effects from SSD background work, and later Magician documentation says Advanced Performance Optimization takes time and can reduce responsiveness on a documented platform.
348. **performance retention ≠ physical-embodiment continuity** — the 2014 Samsung statement says migrated/overwritten data does not show the old-data symptom and the restoration software rewrites old data; the same logical payload can regain service performance through a renewed physical embodiment.
349. **commercial refresh deployment ≠ generic NAND requirement or academic-algorithm identity** — Case 37 grounds one Samsung 840 EVO remediation sequence; it neither proves that all TLC/NAND products require the same policy nor establishes that Samsung implemented Cai et al.'s 2012 FCR algorithms.
350. **retention mechanism presence ≠ retention mechanism readiness** — Intel's S3700/S3500 PLI path can be physically present while capacitor discharge capability remains a separately tested condition; AFh exists because installed protection hardware and current readiness are not the same relation.
351. **retention infrastructure can itself require periodic verification** — Intel explicitly incorporates recurring PLI-circuitry verification over SSD life, so the apparatus intended to perform future failure-time retention work becomes a maintenance target in its own right.
352. **future-fault protection ≠ payload-only retained state** — AEh/AFh retain unclean-shutdown count, last capacitor-test result, time since test, and lifetime test count; a durability regime can depend on operational state about the protection mechanism as well as on user payload.
353. **power-loss event history ≠ protection-capability health** — AEh counts unclean shutdowns regardless of PLI activity, while AFh reports protection self-test health; exposure to a fault class and evidence of preparedness for that class are distinct retained relations.
354. **self-test evidence ≠ whole-device fault validation** — the partial-discharge capacitor self-test checks one bounded readiness property, whereas Intel separately documents switch-timing, hot-unplug/reinsertion, LBA verification, acknowledged-command, and rare-operation validation.
355. **manufacturer validation ≠ independent field compliance** — Intel's documented 7000-repeat PLI validation flow is strong first-party evidence for its validation method, but it does not identify the anonymized FAST '13 devices or independently prove every shipped S3700/S3500 fault outcome.
356. **acknowledged-command durability target ≠ all possible in-flight or higher-layer state** — Intel names no data loss for commands acknowledged complete as a key PLI validation deliverable while separately discussing shorn/unaligned and write-order scenarios; drive validation must not be inflated into filesystem/database crash consistency.
357. **failure endpoint ≠ failure transition envelope** — the S3700 specification conditions full enhanced-power-loss-management functionality on meeting its minimum supply fall time, so a durability contract can depend on how power disappears, not only on the eventual state `power = off`.
358. **automatic self-test ≠ elimination of operational labor** — SMART and SCT move measurement/test machinery into the drive while exposing health, manual invocation, interval control, and replacement decisions to administrators; automation redistributes maintenance responsibility rather than making it vanish.

359. **nonvolatile payload ≠ nonvolatile controller state** — GeckoFTL's power-failure path can leave NAND user pages present while SRAM-resident mapping, directory, cache, and invalidity state disappears and must be reconstructed.
360. **payload survival ≠ immediate restart availability** — the recovery target is explicitly to restore enough SRAM-resident metadata for normal FTL operation to resume; surviving data pages do not by themselves provide the logical service.
361. **metadata persistence ≠ zero recovery work** — Flash-resident mapping/validity structures still require block/OOB scanning, run qualification, directory/GMD reconstruction, dirty-entry recreation, and invalidity-state recovery after interruption.
362. **recovery correctness ≠ recovery latency** — GeckoFTL treats power-failure recovery time as an independent scaling constraint; eventual reconstructability does not by itself satisfy a practical restart-time objective.
363. **more persistent metadata ≠ free durability** — moving controller metadata into Flash can reduce RAM dependence while adding internal I/O, performance cost, and lifetime/endurance pressure; persistence placement is a tradeoff rather than a monotonic good.
364. **physically present metadata ≠ recovery-admissible metadata** — the LSM recovery design rejects runs without completion postambles and discards obsolete runs, so surviving bytes are not enough to make a metadata embodiment current.
365. **surviving data page ≠ recoverable logical identity** — the patent's interrupted-buffer example allows an updated NAND page to remain while loss of its mapping entry makes that page inaccessible until the recovery/checkpoint scheme preserves the relation.
366. **metadata obsolescence ≠ immediately safe erasure** — GeckoFTL can retain an older run until volatile invalidity information is flushed, because an otherwise-obsolete metadata embodiment may still be recovery evidence needed before reclamation is safe.
367. **FTL checkpoint ≠ filesystem/database transaction checkpoint** — GeckoFTL checkpointing bounds unsynchronized mapping state inside the controller; similarity to WAL/journaling is functional only and does not establish application-level atomicity or genealogy.
368. **research evaluation ≠ commercial deployment** — GeckoFTL's peer-reviewed analytical/simulation results ground a research-system retention regime but do not establish that a named shipped SSD used the design or achieved the reported baseline-relative figures.

369. **refresh obligation ≠ one device-wide maintenance cadence** — RAIDR preserves the dynamic-cell restoration obligation while assigning different row classes different refresh intervals; `must refresh` does not imply `refresh every row at the weakest-cell cadence`.
370. **row-retention heterogeneity ≠ temperature-conditioned global scaling** — RAIDR stores row/bin distinctions while separately applying a multiplicative temperature scaler to all rows, so spatial variation and environmental adaptation are independent policy axes.
371. **profile metadata ≠ payload, while profile metadata can be retention infrastructure** — the 2012 design can save profiling results in an OS file and reload them to control later refresh decisions; a description of substrate behavior can therefore become constitutive of retaining the payload.
372. **maintenance-metadata approximation direction matters** — RAIDR's Bloom-filter false positives cause safe over-refresh under the paper's model, whereas an omitted weak-row classification could cause unsafe under-refresh; `approximate` is not one symmetric reliability category.
373. **Bloom-filter no-false-negative property ≠ no profiling false negatives** — the set representation cannot falsely reject an inserted weak row, but the 2013 DPD/VRT evidence shows that measurement can fail to discover a weak state or can later become non-conservative.
374. **row-level retention class ≠ exact per-cell retention policy** — RAIDR defines a row's retention time as the minimum across its cells, so one weak cell can pull an entire row into a faster-maintenance class.
375. **measured retention time ≠ guaranteed future minimum retention time** — the 2013 study reports VRT transitions below prior measurements and states that even a 2x safety margin may be insufficient in the presence of VRT.
376. **retention profile ≠ immutable physical truth** — DPD makes measured retention depend on stored patterns and neighboring state, while VRT makes some cell behavior change over time; a profile is an empirical policy input, not a timeless cell constant.
377. **control-state survival ≠ control-state validity** — a saved retention profile can survive across boots while becoming stale or having been incomplete from the start; retaining maintenance metadata is insufficient unless its represented relation remains conservative.
378. **more selective maintenance ≠ zero profiling/control cost** — RAIDR reduces restoration operations by adding profiling, retained bin/Bloom-filter state, counters, hashing, row-specific commands, and temperature scaling.
379. **retention-aware refresh policy ≠ self-refresh authority** — the 2012 paper separately models DRAM self refresh as internally managed, while RAIDR keeps row-selection policy on the controller side and wakes DRAM for selected refreshes.
380. **research evaluation ≠ commercial deployment** — RAIDR's reported refresh/power/performance/storage figures are research evaluation results; the source set does not establish a shipped controller using RAIDR or a JEDEC per-row retention-profile contract.

381. **logical deletion ≠ immediate physical removal** — Cassandra 3.11 explicitly writes a tombstone rather than immediately removing underlying data; the user-visible delete and retirement of all old embodiments are different events.
382. **negative-state retention ≠ positive-payload retention** — a tombstone is retained so an older positive value will no longer count as current; preserving the fact of deletion and preserving the deleted payload are opposite operational roles even though both consume retained state.
383. **tombstone presence ≠ replica convergence** — a newer tombstone can coexist with a disconnected replica that still holds the older value; negative currentness remains a local/distributed relation until delivery or repair propagates it.
384. **`gc_grace_seconds` expiry ≠ immediate tombstone reclamation** — age only makes a tombstone eligible; Cassandra still requires a compaction event and overlap safety relative to older shadowed SSTable data.
385. **retention window ≠ repair guarantee** — the grace interval preserves anti-resurrection evidence but does not itself contact or reconcile a failed replica; repair remains separate maintenance work.
386. **hint retention ≠ tombstone retention ≠ anti-entropy repair** — Cassandra 3.11 calls hints best effort, gives them their own downtime window, and explicitly says they do not replace anti-entropy repair; similar-looking retained-control states have different guarantees and triggers.
387. **forgetting deletion evidence can resurrect older payload** — Apache's documented failure example shows that if the tombstone is gone while a stale replica retains the older value, later repair can make that value reappear across the cluster.
388. **repair can preserve forgetting or defeat it depending on surviving currentness evidence** — repair propagates the tombstone when deletion evidence survives, but can propagate the stale value when it does not; `repair` is not semantically independent of the states admitted as current inputs.
389. **repair-qualified forgetting ≠ cost-free safety** — `only_purge_repaired_tombstones` can delay purge until repaired-state evidence exists, while Apache's release note warns that failing to repair for long periods can retain tombstones long enough to cause other operational problems.
390. **tombstone reclamation ≠ secure erasure** — removing Cassandra's negative currentness marker is a distributed-storage reclamation event; it does not establish sanitization of every stale physical copy or lower-layer media trace.

391. **current-state reconstructability ≠ complete history retention** — Kafka 0.8.1 explicitly uses compaction to retain at least the final value for every key without retaining a complete log of all changes; being able to rebuild `now` does not imply being able to replay every prior state.
392. **stable logical offset ≠ surviving record at that offset** — compacted-away records do not cause offset renumbering; Apache says the offset remains a valid position and a read continues from the next surviving offset.
393. **ordering continuity ≠ dense record occupancy** — compaction never reorders surviving messages but deliberately creates holes where older records were removed; ordered traversal and complete occupancy are separate relations.
394. **stable logical position ≠ stable physical byte position** — the 0.8.1 segment source explicitly maps logical offsets to physical file positions while the cleaner recopies and swaps segment embodiments; logical position can remain fixed while physical placement changes.
395. **compaction-mediated forgetting ≠ in-place record erasure** — Kafka 0.8.1 forgets superseded history by constructing cleaned segments that copy survivors and omit obsolete records, making preservation of the current state and physical reconstruction of its carrier one coupled operation.
396. **delete-marker retention ≠ indefinite negative-state retention** — a null-payload delete marker first defeats older same-key values, but Kafka deliberately removes the marker itself after its configured retention relation so negative state does not accumulate forever.
397. **observer progress can be part of a retention guarantee** — Apache guarantees that a start-to-head consumer sees all delete markers only if it catches up within `delete.retention.ms`; whether the negative state remains observable can depend on reader traversal time while cleanup proceeds concurrently.
398. **Kafka `delete.retention.ms` ≠ Cassandra `gc_grace_seconds` ≠ Swift `reclaim_age`** — all three can bound negative-state lifetime, but Kafka's bounded relation is compacted-log consumer observation, Cassandra's is stale-replica/repair anti-resurrection safety, and Swift's is timestamped replica/reconstruction convergence; vocabulary similarity does not establish mechanism identity.
399. **compaction ≠ time-based retention** — Kafka 0.8.1 explicitly distinguishes per-key compaction from coarse age/size deletion: one retains a latest keyed state even when old, while the other can discard old records regardless of whether they are the only surviving state for a key.
400. **source-of-current-state role ≠ point-in-time archive role** — a compacted Kafka topic can remain a source from which current keyed state is rebuilt while no longer retaining enough superseded records to reproduce arbitrary historical points; `source of truth` for current state does not mean complete historical archive.

401. **profile survival ≠ policy validity, and runtime fault evidence can revise maintenance policy** — AVATAR does not trust the initial row profile as an immutable future truth: a correctable runtime error can change the affected row's retained refresh classification.
402. **error correction ≠ future protection** — ECC restores the currently observed logical word, while the subsequent row upgrade changes how aggressively the row will be refreshed in the future; repair of one manifestation and revision of later maintenance are separate actions.
403. **demand-triggered ECC observation ≠ full-memory coverage** — ordinary accesses expose errors only in touched lines, so AVATAR adds proactive scrub to inspect cold memory before treating runtime feedback as sufficiently broad.
404. **memory scrub ≠ DRAM refresh** — scrub reads/checks/corrects and supplies classification evidence, whereas refresh restores dynamic-cell charge according to the resulting cadence; the two forms of maintenance compose without becoming one mechanism.
405. **ECC correction event ≠ certain VRT diagnosis** — the paper notes that soft errors can also trigger conservative row upgrades, so a safe policy response need not imply perfect causal classification of the observed fault.
406. **conservative runtime upgrade ≠ irreversible classification** — AVATAR can accumulate Fast-Refresh upgrades after errors, then use infrequent retention re-testing to downgrade rows again; second-order maintenance state has a revision lifecycle rather than one permanent label.
407. **less refresh work ≠ less total maintenance work** — refresh savings are purchased with ECC checking/correction, RRT state, periodic whole-memory scrub, runtime policy updates, and infrequent re-testing; optimization redistributes maintenance rather than abolishing it.
408. **model-level reliability result ≠ deployed hardware lifetime** — AVATAR's long time-to-failure estimates combine 24-chip VRT characterization with an architecture-level model and assumptions; they are not decades of commercial field observation.
409. **scrub-coverage assumption ≠ demonstrated universal detection** — the paper explicitly assumes that scrub identifies VRT-related errors arising within its interval; this closes the evaluation model but does not prove that every real scrub implementation catches every possible interval failure.
410. **online VRT adaptation ≠ commercial retention-aware-refresh deployment** — the 2015 paper grounds a research architecture and evaluation, not a named shipped memory controller, processor, DIMM, or JEDEC feature implementing AVATAR.
411. **retention-policy state can itself require revalidation** — the Row Refresh Table is retained control state used to decide future maintenance, yet runtime upgrades and later re-testing show that this second-order state must itself be kept current enough to remain safe and efficient.

412. **logical deallocation ≠ physical/media erasure** — NVMe 1.3 permits a deallocated logical block to return the last data written, so ending the allocation obligation does not establish that the prior medium embodiment was erased, overwritten, or cryptographically severed;
413. **host no-longer-needs hint ≠ executed reclamation** — Dataset Management is explicitly advisory and a compliant controller may choose to take no action; host intent and controller/media work are separate relations;
414. **deallocated logical block ≠ pre-sanitize unreadability** — the deallocated-read contract allows zeroes, `FFh`, or the last-written value, whereas a successful sanitize separately prohibits access to data and metadata written before that operation;

416. **sanitize command completion ≠ sanitize-operation completion** — Block Erase, Crypto Erase, and Overwrite execute in the background; successful command completion establishes that the operation started, while completion is represented later through sanitize status/event state;

419. **sanitize persistence across reset ≠ ordinary I/O availability** — Revision 1.3 makes a started sanitize non-abortable and continuing across controller reset/power cycles while ordinary commands are restricted; continuation of forgetting work and continuation of normal service are separate targets;

447. **logical invisibility ≠ digital sanitization** — FAST ’11 explicitly separates ordinary-interface disappearance from inability to recover old data by lower-level digital means; FTL-remapped stale pages can survive after the old value ceases to be current through the host interface;

450. **complete logical-address overwrite ≠ complete physical-witness overwrite** — repeated writes across every visible LBA can still miss remapped, stale, or over-provisioned Flash embodiments;
451. **one current logical value ≠ one surviving physical embodiment** — an FTL can expose one current LBA value while multiple older digital copies survive underneath it without constituting an intentional version archive;

548. **activity accounting ≠ complete access history** — a rolling accumulated ACT budget retains enough pressure evidence for maintenance decisions without preserving an archival sequence of every activation;
549. **controller RFM issuance ≠ in-DRAM mitigation algorithm** — the public command grants internal management time without exposing the device's complete victim-selection, topology, or restoration implementation;
550. **host-visible bank accounting ≠ physical victim-row knowledge** — the controller can police a bank-level activation budget while physical adjacency and exact victim selection remain hidden inside the DRAM;
551. **standard/interface support ≠ enabled platform behavior** — Intel document 743844 Rev. 015 (May 2025) says RFM is supported according to JEDEC while separately stating that DDR5 RFM is not yet enabled on the bounded platform family;
552. **DDR5 RFM support ≠ RFM requirement for every DDR5 device** — a Micron 16Gb DDR5 die-revision addendum explicitly records `RFM not required`, so the memory-generation label alone does not determine the extra-maintenance obligation;
553. **absence of RFM command ≠ absence of all RowHammer mitigation** — McSee observed no RFM commands on its tested Intel platforms but did observe additional mitigative activations, so command absence cannot be equated with zero defense;
554. **platform mitigation presence ≠ conformance to a device-required RFM path** — an alternative controller defense and a DRAM's advertised RFM requirement are separate relations whose equivalence requires empirical validation rather than assumption;
555. **documented capability ≠ observed execution** — manufacturer/platform documents define requirements and enablement, while McSee's bus capture separately tests what commands a running controller actually emits;
556. **independent bus observation ≠ universal vendor/platform guarantee** — McSee's Intel/AMD result is strong for its tested CPUs, firmware/configurations, and DIMM pool but cannot be projected onto every DDR5 system;
557. **retention work can be protocol-composed across incomplete authorities** — the device can expose requirement/threshold state, the controller can retain activity pressure and schedule a command, and the DRAM can perform opaque internal work; no one layer alone supplies the whole maintenance relation;
558. **DDR5 RFM evolution ≠ origin of RowHammer-aware targeted refresh** — Case 53's 2012-priority Intel evidence already predates DDR5 RFM, so the later contribution here is the controller/device responsibility split rather than an invention claim.
559. **retained device-health state ≠ retained user payload** — NVMe 1.0e explicitly defines SMART/Health information over the life of the controller and retained across power cycles, so an SSD can preserve non-payload state about its own use/condition;
560. **retained lifetime counter ≠ complete device history** — host data, power-cycle/hour, unsafe-shutdown, and error counters preserve category totals without retaining event order, per-LBA sequence, causality, or the controller's complete internal media history;
561. **vendor-specific endurance estimate ≠ direct measurement of every cell's remaining life** — `Percentage Used` is normatively an estimate based on actual usage plus the manufacturer's prediction of NVM life, not a disclosed per-cell physical-wear census;
562. **100% Percentage Used ≠ device failure** — NVMe 1.0e and 1.3 explicitly allow the estimate to reach/exceed 100 while warning that 100 may not indicate device/subsystem failure;
563. **host Data Units Written ≠ physical NAND program/erase work** — the standardized counter measures data the host wrote to the controller and excludes metadata; it is not normatively a count of internal page programs, block erases, GC copies, mapping writes, or write amplification;
564. **Available Spare ≠ user-addressable free capacity** — the field describes remaining spare capacity for the device, while user-visible namespace/filesystem free space is a different address/capacity relation;
565. **spare-threshold crossing ≠ immediate payload loss** — the threshold can produce a health/asynchronous notification before the interface asserts that current user data has become unreadable; warning margin and present payload availability remain distinct;
566. **current Critical Warning state ≠ retained cumulative health history** — NVMe 1.3 explicitly says Critical Warning bits describe the current associated state and are nonpersistent even though the surrounding SMART/Health interface contains cumulative/lifetime information;
567. **one SMART/Health log page ≠ one temporal regime** — current warning bits, retained counters, reserve state, instantaneous/composite temperature, and a model-derived endurance estimate coexist in one structure without sharing the same persistence semantics;
568. **unrecovered media/data-integrity error count ≠ all ECC/correction work** — the NVMe field counts controller-detected unrecovered integrity errors, so successful correction or raw-error activity cannot be inferred from that counter alone;
569. **telemetry update cadence ≠ media-maintenance cadence** — `Percentage Used` has a specified update interval, but that cadence is not the schedule for NAND refresh, garbage collection, wear leveling, read reclaim, bad-block replacement, or host retirement;
570. **stable logical capacity ≠ absence of hidden maintenance capacity** — Intel's 2014 P3700 document keeps user LBA count stable while separately reserving some physical capacity for NAND management/maintenance; the logical namespace need not visibly shrink as reserve is consumed;
571. **hidden maintenance capacity ≠ automatically identical to NVMe Available Spare** — the P3700 capacity note proves non-user management reserve exists, but the bounded sources do not establish a byte-for-byte identity between every hidden physical reserve and the standardized Available Spare percentage;
572. **health telemetry can qualify future service without itself preserving the payload** — cumulative errors, remaining spare, and endurance estimates can guide investigation/replacement while the physical retention/refresh/remapping mechanisms remain separate layers;
573. **generic NVMe SMART/Health telemetry ≠ product-specific PLI readiness validation** — Case 38's Intel capacitor self-test/event semantics are a distinct health relation; a generic standardized endurance log does not prove a particular backup-power subsystem has been self-tested or is ready;
574. **NVMe 1.3 clarification ≠ invention of NVMe endurance telemetry** — official 1.0e already contains the core SMART/Health endurance fields and Intel's 2014 P3700 implements the mandatory NVMe 1.0 log, so 1.3 is used here for a persistence-boundary clarification rather than an origin claim.

**Grounded filesystem crash-admissibility bridge:** [`cases/16-bsd-ffs-soft-updates-crash-admissibility.md`](cases/16-bsd-ffs-soft-updates-crash-admissibility.md) is `grounded`. McKusick/Ganger 1999 and Ganger et al. 2000 directly ground production 4.4BSD FFS soft updates as per-field/pointer dependency tracking with delayed writeback, temporary rollback/roll-forward of still-dependent updates, immediate safe post-crash mountability with bounded residual resource leaks, and a distinct `fsync` path whose stable-storage closure reaches beyond payload blocks into allocation, indirection, inode, and naming metadata. The bridge adds `application-visible currentness ≠ crash-admissible stable state`, `crash consistency ≠ newest-operation durability`, and `filesystem durability closure ≠ device flush boundary`; journaling, copy-on-write/checkpoint recovery, modern `fsync`/rename contracts, and lower-layer persistence composition remain separate regimes.

**Grounded RAID parity-reconstruction bridge:** [`cases/17-raid-parity-reconstruction-degraded-repair.md`](cases/17-raid-parity-reconstruction-degraded-repair.md) is `grounded`. Ouchi’s 1977-filed IBM patent directly grounds segmented records, XOR `check sum`, failure-independent placement, and reconstruction of one unavailable contribution; Patterson/Gibson/Katz establish the later `RAID` terminology; Chen et al. ground array `meta state`, stale-data invalidation, parity consistency state, demand reconstruction, stand-by spares, and full background reconstruction. The bridge adds `parity redundancy ≠ replica multiplicity`, `single-failure service continuity ≠ restored redundancy margin`, `parity presence ≠ parity currentness`, and `repair progress/spare capacity can be constitutive retention infrastructure`; later RAID-6/rebuild policy remain separate regimes.

**Grounded proactive-scrubbing bridge:** [`cases/18-zfs-scrub-latent-error-detection.md`](cases/18-zfs-scrub-latent-error-detection.md) is `grounded`; see [`evidence/18-zfs-scrub-2004-2010-grounding.md`](evidence/18-zfs-scrub-2004-2010-grounding.md). Official Solaris ZFS documentation grounds pool-wide scrub, filesystem-layer checksums, redundant-copy self-healing, and replacement-triggered resilvering; Schwarz et al. 2004 controls scrubbing invention priority; independent latent-sector-error work supplies the defect-before-discovery context. The case adds `physical presence/readability ≠ verified integrity`, `redundancy availability ≠ defect discovery`, `detection work ≠ repair work`, and `scrub ≠ rebuild/resilver`, while rejecting a DRAM-refresh equivalence.

**Grounded distributed-erasure-coding bridge:** [`cases/19-facebook-f4-erasure-coded-failure-domains.md`](cases/19-facebook-f4-erasure-coded-failure-domains.md) is `grounded`; see [`evidence/19-facebook-f4-2014-erasure-coding-grounding.md`](evidence/19-facebook-f4-2014-erasure-coding-grounding.md). The 2014 f4 production paper directly grounds immutable warm-BLOB data, Reed–Solomon `(10,4)` data/parity stripes, separately triple-replicated index files, rack-aware placement, online requested-BLOB reconstruction, offline full-block rebuilding, placement balancing after failure/reconstruction/replacement, and a geo-XOR layer whose inputs can themselves require local reconstruction. The bridge adds `erasure-code algebra ≠ failure-domain independence`, `requested-object availability ≠ completed repair`, `content reconstruction ≠ restored placement geometry`, and `reconstruction can compose across coding/failure-domain layers`, while f4’s own Related Work blocks a false erasure-code-invention narrative.

## Case 56 — replicated-log committed-prefix findings

575. **Physical record presence ≠ committed retention.** Kafka 0.8.2 can retain records above the high watermark in a local log while ordinary consumers remain bounded to the committed prefix.
576. **Leader log-end offset ≠ committed frontier.** The leader may possess a longer suffix than the slowest current ISR replica; the high watermark advances from the minimum ISR log-end frontier.
577. **Replication factor ≠ current redundancy margin.** Assigned replicas describe intended multiplicity; ISR membership describes currently qualified replication participants.
578. **Assigned replica membership ≠ replica currentness.** A physically present assigned replica can be removed from ISR when it is too far behind or stuck.
579. **Follower catch-up ≠ immediate currentness until the ISR condition is satisfied.** In the bounded source, a follower can re-enter ISR only after reaching at least the leader high watermark plus the assignment/membership checks.
580. **Follower-replication visibility ≠ ordinary consumer visibility.** Replica fetches may copy the leader tail beyond the high watermark because that copying is how commitment can advance; ordinary consumer reads are capped at the high watermark.
581. **Follower log end ≠ follower committed prefix.** The follower sets its high watermark to the minimum of its own log end and the leader-reported high watermark.
582. **Retained high-watermark checkpoint ≠ retained replication history.** `replication-offset-checkpoint` preserves a recovery boundary without recording every fetch, lag transition, or ISR change that produced it.
583. **Runtime high watermark ≠ necessarily the most recently checkpointed high watermark at every instant.** The 0.8.2 configuration explicitly checkpoints periodically; this distinction does not by itself imply data loss because recovery uses more than one relation.
584. **`request.required.acks=-1` ≠ acknowledgement by every assigned replica.** In the bounded regime it composes with the current ISR/high-watermark path and `min.insync.replicas`.
585. **Longer surviving log ≠ more authoritative log.** The unclean-election recovery path can truncate a returning follower whose local end lies beyond the current leader end.
586. **Failover convergence can require deliberate forgetting.** Truncating an extant divergent suffix is a protocol repair/convergence operation, not evidence that those bytes were physically absent.
587. **Unclean leader election availability ≠ the normal committed-message retention guarantee.** Electing a non-ISR replica can restore service while explicitly accepting possible data loss.
588. **Kafka replication truncation ≠ Kafka log compaction.** Case 42 forgets superseded committed keyed history; Case 56 can forget a non-authoritative/divergent suffix during replica convergence.
589. **Kafka high watermark/ISR ≠ generic quorum or consensus semantics.** Kafka's own design situates the mechanism among earlier replicated-log work and names PacificA as close prior art; the project keeps the 0.8.2 implementation specific rather than turning all commit-frontier mechanisms into one genealogy.

## Case 57 — Bigtable redo/materialization findings

590. **Committed mutation ≠ surviving memtable object.** Bigtable records a valid mutation in the commit log and commits it before inserting the same update into the volatile memtable, so RAM-object survival is not the durability condition for a committed mutation.
591. **Current readable tablet state ≠ one current physical file.** Reads merge the memtable with the live SSTables; the current tablet is a compositional view rather than one privileged file embodiment.
592. **Persistent logical state can include reconstructible volatile working state.** The memtable participates in ordinary serving while its committed contents can be recreated after tablet-server loss from retained persistent evidence.
593. **Recovery metadata ≠ payload, while recovery metadata can be retention infrastructure.** The live SSTable list and redo points do not themselves carry the user values, yet they qualify which surviving files/log ranges must be combined to reconstruct the tablet.
594. **Redo point ≠ redo history.** A redo point is a compact boundary into retained mutation history, not a record of every mutation or every recovery event.
595. **Minor compaction ≠ original commit/durability event.** Mutations can already be committed through the log before a frozen memtable is materialized as an SSTable.
596. **Current-state materialization can reduce future history-retention obligation.** Bigtable explicitly uses minor compaction to reduce the amount of commit log that recovery must read; after safe materialization, the same old redo prefix need not remain constitutive of restart.
597. **One physical commit log ≠ one logical tablet history.** A tablet server shares one log among tablets, and recovery sorts co-mingled records back by table/row/sequence rather than treating physical append adjacency as logical-tablet identity.
598. **Duplicate retained redo records ≠ duplicate logical mutation application.** Bigtable's log-switching optimization can leave duplicates, while sequence numbers let recovery elide them.
599. **Immutable SSTable survival ≠ current tablet membership.** SSTable files do not change in place, but tablet `METADATA` names the live set and obsolete files can later be garbage-collected.
600. **Deletion entry ≠ deleted payload physically absent.** A delete marker can make an older value noncurrent while that older value still remains in a live SSTable.
601. **Major-compaction forgetting ≠ raw-media sanitization.** Bigtable major compaction can omit deleted data and deletion entries from the new live representation without proving erasure from GFS replicas, disks, backups, or forensic layers.
602. **Bigtable compaction ≠ Kafka log compaction.** Both rewrite retained representations, but Bigtable's memtable/SSTable compaction has no cited Kafka-style permanent logical-offset contract.
603. **Bigtable commit log ≠ Kafka replicated log/high watermark.** Bigtable uses redo to reconstruct tablet working state; Case 56 uses ISR/high-watermark state to qualify a committed prefix of a replicated append log.
604. **Bigtable 2006 LSM/logging composition ≠ invention of LSM/logging/group commit.** Chang et al. explicitly compare the memtable/SSTable design to the 1996 LSM-tree and cite earlier logging/group-commit literature; the grounded claim is the Bigtable-specific composition and retention relation.

## Case 58 — Raft snapshot/log-compaction findings

605. **Committed state ≠ indefinitely retained command history.** Raft can preserve the result of a committed prefix in a stable state-machine snapshot and later delete the original covered entries.
606. **Snapshot current-state payload ≠ complete protocol-continuation state.** `last included index`, `last included term`, and the effective cluster configuration must accompany the materialized state so replication/membership semantics can continue after the source entries disappear.
607. **Snapshot boundary ≠ history it replaces.** The index/term locate and qualify a compacted prefix; they do not preserve the detailed command sequence of that prefix.
608. **Completed replacement snapshot precedes covered-prefix retirement.** The bounded paper authorizes deletion once snapshot writing completes, making deletion a representation handoff rather than blind history loss.
609. **Log compaction ≠ original commitment.** Consensus commitment and application happen before the later maintenance operation that materializes and retires old history.
610. **Compacted replay prefix ≠ unrecoverable follower.** A follower can remain recoverable after needed log entries disappear if an authoritative snapshot is available for transfer.
611. **History compaction can change the future repair protocol.** A lagging follower ordinarily receives `AppendEntries`; once it falls behind compacted history, repair changes to `InstallSnapshot` state transfer.
612. **Follower lag ≠ data loss.** Lag becomes a recovery-path problem only in relation to the leader's retained-history frontier and available snapshot representation.
613. **Physically surviving follower log ≠ consensus authority.** Installing a newer snapshot can require discarding an entire existing local log when it is superseded or may contain conflicting uncommitted entries.
614. **Compatible post-snapshot suffix ≠ superseded prefix.** If the follower has an entry matching the snapshot's last included index and term, later entries may remain valid even while covered entries are deleted.
615. **Configuration history entry can disappear while effective membership state must survive.** Raft carries the latest configuration as of the snapshot boundary into the snapshot itself.
616. **Snapshot cadence trades retained-history/replay cost against present materialization work.** Too-frequent snapshots waste bandwidth/energy; too-infrequent snapshots grow storage and restart replay cost.
617. **Authoritative committed-prefix forgetting ≠ divergent-suffix truncation.** Raft Case 58 retires already-committed history after state-equivalence handoff; Kafka Case 56 can truncate a nonauthoritative divergent suffix after failover.
618. **Raft snapshotting ≠ GFS checkpointing ≠ Bigtable compaction.** All can substitute materialized state for replay history at a functional level, but their logs, authority rules, metadata, and recovery protocols differ.
619. **Raft log-prefix deletion ≠ secure erase.** Removal from Raft's live recovery representation does not prove lower-layer media sanitization or forensic absence.
620. **Raft 2014 snapshotting ≠ invention of snapshotting/log compaction.** The paper itself names Chubby and ZooKeeper as snapshot users and discusses log cleaning/LSM alternatives; the grounded claim is the Raft-specific consensus/snapshot retention composition.

## Case 59 — NAND program-interference findings

621. **Successful aggressor programming ≠ unchanged neighboring retained state.** In the bounded planar MLC regime, programming a neighboring floating gate can capacitively shift an already-programmed victim threshold distribution even though the victim was not the write target.
622. **Logical write target ≠ complete physical electrical-effect scope.** Host/page selection names the intended update, while parasitic coupling determines a wider physical side-effect geometry.
623. **Logical page independence ≠ physical retention independence.** Two pages can be separately addressable while the reliability margin of one depends on later programming of a physical neighbor.
624. **Program-order history can matter even when elapsed retention age is held aside.** The 2013 tests show materially different victim interference under in-page-order and tested out-of-page-order programming.
625. **Same final set of programmed pages ≠ necessarily the same interference history.** Which neighboring LSB/MSB transitions occur after the victim can change how many interference events it receives; this remains bounded to the measured two-bit MLC regime.
626. **Neighbor data value can affect victim retention margin without becoming victim payload.** Larger aggressor threshold transitions can induce larger coupled victim shifts, so physical recoverability is relational without redefining logical identity.
627. **Physical adjacency/coupling geometry ≠ logical namespace adjacency.** Wordline/direct-neighbor relations dominate the bounded measurements; controller-visible logical designations alone do not describe the electrical coupling relation.
628. **Program interference ≠ retention-age leakage.** Cai et al. explicitly separate neighbor-program-induced errors from gradual retention charge loss even though both consume raw-error/ECC margin.
629. **Program interference ≠ read disturb.** Case 52 accumulates pass-voltage stress through reads; Case 59 couples an aggressor programming transition into a victim. Shared cross-target side effects do not imply one mechanism or maintenance clock.
630. **Cell-to-cell program interference ≠ every use of `program disturb`.** Related NAND literature uses program-disturb vocabulary for other inhibited/unselected-cell program-path failures; mechanism must be checked before terminology is merged.
631. **Read-reference recovery ≠ physical-state restoration.** Adjusting the discrimination voltage can recover the intended logical value from a shifted victim distribution without returning the floating-gate threshold to its pre-interference position.
632. **Recovery criterion can adapt to medium history.** The 2013 controller proposal learns/predicts post-interference distributions and changes later read boundaries; reliable continuation can therefore depend on interpretation policy as well as the retained charge state.
633. **Neighbor retained state can become decoding side information.** The 2014 NAC proposal conditions reread references on immediate-neighbor values after ECC failure, using another retained value to improve recovery of the victim.
634. **Research mitigation evaluation ≠ named-controller deployment.** The 2013 dynamic-reference and 2014 NAC results are experimental/model/simulation evidence and must not be upgraded into claims about shipped SSD firmware.
635. **2013 commercial-chip characterization ≠ invention of NAND cell-to-cell interference or program-order mitigation.** Lee et al. document floating-gate interference in 2002, and Samsung-linked 2007–2008 work already treats cell-to-cell coupling/program architecture/order as a mitigation problem.

## Case 60 — Apollo core-rope findings

636. **State-bearing structure ≠ state-changing transducer.** In the bounded AGC fixed-memory regime, the program bit is encoded by a sense conductor's thread/bypass relation while the selected ferrite core intentionally switches during readout.
637. **Ferrite-core switching during read ≠ destructive logical read.** The selected core is set and reset, but the fixed program remains because the bit-defining topology is not rewritten by that magnetic cycle.
638. **Use of magnetic material ≠ magnetic-state payload retention.** Case 02 and Case 60 both use ferrite switching physics, yet one retains the bit in remanent polarity and the other uses the core principally as a transformer/read transducer for wired geometry.
639. **Read-only at runtime ≠ physically immutable artifact.** Ordinary AGC program steps cannot rewrite rope contents, while humans can manufacture, rework, replace, damage, or destroy the module.
640. **Program supersession ≠ physical disappearance of the superseded program artifact.** A replaced rope can remain fully readable even though another installed module has become the current flight program.
641. **Manufacturing correctness can be information correctness.** In a geometry-coded memory, a routing error can directly become a wrong stored bit rather than merely a packaging defect around an otherwise correct state.
642. **Write semantics can migrate from runtime command to fabrication workflow.** Changing the fixed program means producing a new physical wiring configuration rather than issuing an ordinary electrical write instruction.
643. **Low quiescent retention work ≠ low lifecycle retention labor.** Core rope needs no periodic payload refresh merely to keep the fixed program, while substantial labor moves into bit-pattern preparation, routing, assembly, verification, configuration control, and replacement.
644. **Revision latency ≠ read latency ≠ retention interval.** NASA's bounded Apollo witness of an approximately four-week program-change production cycle describes update/replacement time, not the time to read a word or a universal shelf-retention duration.
645. **Payload topology ≠ recoverability infrastructure.** Sense-line routing carries fixed bits, while set/reset, inhibit, diode/switching, and sense-selection paths determine whether the encoded relation can be addressed and recovered.
646. **One ferrite core ≠ one stored bit.** The bounded Block II rope organization lets many sense lines thread or bypass one selected core, so a core participates in many fixed bits rather than carrying a single bit through its own remanence.
647. **Shared material family ≠ shared retention semantics.** Calling both Case 02 and Case 60 `core memory` is insufficient to characterize what remains, what read changes, and what operation constitutes rewrite.
648. **Manufactured fixed pattern ≈ mask ROM only as a functional analogy.** Both can expose ordinary-runtime read-only state, but core-rope magnetic transformer coupling and semiconductor mask-ROM fabrication are different mechanisms; no direct genealogy is established here.
649. **Apollo core rope ≠ invention of wired-in fixed memory.** Nelson's 1964-filed wired-core patent and MIT's own 1966-filed wired-in-memory manufacturing patent place geometry-coded fixed memories in a broader contemporary prior-art field.
650. **Reduced accidental runtime rewrite risk ≠ maintenance-free or revision-free retention.** Fixedness suppresses one class of in-service modification by moving change authority and correctness work into manufacturing, verification, module logistics, and physical replacement.

667. **Ordered catch-up ≠ divergent-version reconciliation.** HDFS Observer waits for one journal-ordered namespace replica to reach a frontier; Dynamo Case 23 may retain concurrent versions that require reconciliation.
668. **HDFS-12943 ≠ invention of consistent follower reads.** The supported historical claim is the Apache-specific composition of Observer role, transaction-backed state ID, `msync`, alignment, routing/fallback, and faster edit tailing.

685. **high watermark ≠ last stable offset** — Kafka 0.11 keeps the replication committed-prefix frontier distinct from the transaction-sensitive `READ_COMMITTED` stability frontier;

691. **first open transaction can constrain visibility of later offsets** — an unresolved earlier transaction can hold LSO behind later records that already exist and may already be replicated;
692. **offset-order preservation can turn one open transaction into a partition-wide read frontier** — later records can wait behind an earlier open transaction without belonging to it;

700. **Kafka 0.11 transactional isolation ≠ invention of transactions/atomic commit** — System R and broader database transaction/log-recovery literature long predate Kafka; the bounded historical claim is the Kafka-specific composition of replicated log, hidden decision markers, LSO, aborted-range indexing, and selectable read isolation.
