# RAID Parity Reconstruction: Reconstructability, Degraded Operation, and Repair Margin

## Scope

- **Bounded mechanism:** parity/checksum-coded disk-group retention under one member failure, with validity/currentness state and reconstruction onto spare capacity.
- **Bounded period:** 1977–1994.
- **Primary prior-art witness:** Norman Ken Ouchi / IBM, US4092732A, **“System for recovering data stored in failed memory unit,”** filed 31 May 1977, published 30 May 1978.
- **RAID terminology witness:** David A. Patterson, Garth Gibson, and Randy H. Katz, **“A Case for Redundant Arrays of Inexpensive Disks (RAID),”** UCB/CSD-87-391 (December 1987) / SIGMOD 1988.
- **Primary period mechanism/implementation witness:** Peter M. Chen, Edward K. Lee, Garth A. Gibson, Randy H. Katz, and David A. Patterson, **“RAID: High-Performance, Reliable Secondary Storage,”** UCB/CSD-93-778 (November 1993), later _ACM Computing Surveys_ 26(2), June 1994.
- **Research question:** what exactly survives when a parity-protected disk member fails, and why is successful degraded service still different from restored redundancy?

This is **not** a general history of RAID levels. It does not attempt to settle the full genealogy of mirroring, striping, parity, RAID-5 implementation, RAID-6/P+Q, controller products, or modern rebuild policy. It also does not cover periodic **scrubbing** as a distinct latent-error-detection regime. The bounded comparison is narrower:

> **A logical data contribution may remain reconstructable after its physical member disappears, yet the array can simultaneously have less future failure margin and require failure-triggered repair before its original redundancy condition is restored.**

The case therefore adds a different kind of retention from Case 05’s replicated RADOS objects and Case 14’s single-disk defect reassignment. The missing material contribution need not survive anywhere as a full duplicate: it can be **reconstructed from a coded relation among the surviving members**.

## Historical record

### H/P — parity/checksum recovery predates the RAID name

Ouchi’s IBM patent, filed in 1977, explicitly contrasts full duplication with a less storage-expensive reconstruction scheme. A record is divided into segments. A same-length `check sum` is generated by `Exclusive Oring` the record segments, and the segments plus check sum are placed on different `failure independent memory units`. If one unit is unavailable, the surviving segments plus the check sum can reconstruct the unavailable segment.

The patent’s example uses five storage units for four record segments plus one check-sum segment. Its disclosed system uses disk drives and a directory that records where segments are physically stored.

This establishes a strict prior-art boundary:

> Patterson, Gibson, and Katz’s 1987–1988 work is primary evidence for the **RAID** name and its level taxonomy, but it is not evidence that parity/checksum reconstruction across multiple storage devices began with that name.

**Primary anchor:** Ouchi, US4092732A, Definitions/description, especially the discussion of duplicate storage versus segmented records and `check sum`, and the reconstruction description around the check-sum generator.

### H/P — RAID makes redundancy an array-level organization rather than merely a second copy

Patterson, Gibson, and Katz’s Berkeley report (December 1987) and SIGMOD paper (June 1988) explicitly introduce `RAID` and organize several redundancy schemes as levels. The paper’s project is broader than this case—performance, cost, reliability, and level comparison—but its nomenclature fixes the period term that later literature uses.

For this bounded case, the important point is negative as much as positive: **RAID redundancy is not synonymous with mirroring.** The historical RAID taxonomy already contains organizations where check/parity information, rather than another full copy of every data block, supplies failure tolerance.

**Primary/institutional anchors:** UCB/CSD-87-391; Patterson, Gibson, Katz, SIGMOD 1988, pp. 109–116.

### H/P — correct operation needs retained state beyond payload and parity

Chen et al. state in §3.5 that the necessary state of a disk array consists of more than the data and parity stored on disk. The implementation must accurately maintain information about:

- which disks have failed;
- how much of a failed disk has already been reconstructed;
- which sectors are currently being updated.

They call this non-user-data, non-parity information `meta state`.

This is a direct retention result, not merely an implementation footnote. A set of surviving data/parity sectors does not by itself tell the array which contributions are currently admissible or how far repair has progressed.

**Primary anchor:** Chen et al., UCB/CSD-93-778, §3.5, PDF pp. 29–30.

### H/P — validity metadata prevents stale physical survivors from masquerading as current data

Chen et al. require the logical sectors of a failed disk to be marked invalid **before** requests that would normally access them are attempted. This prevents users from reading stale/corrupted material from a disk that is considered failed but may work intermittently.

When an invalid logical sector has been reconstructed onto a spare disk, the logical sector must be marked valid before later writes to that logical location are serviced, ensuring that subsequent writes update the reconstructed embodiment rather than leaving it stale.

They explicitly say both restrictions are needed to prevent users from receiving stale data, and they discuss storing valid/invalid state in a bit vector on stable storage.

So physical readability after failure is not enough:

> **surviving bytes ≠ authorized current contribution**.

**Primary anchor:** Chen et al., UCB/CSD-93-778, §3.5.1, PDF p. 30.

### H/P — parity can physically survive while ceasing to be trustworthy

Chen et al. describe a crash during writes as capable of leaving parity inconsistent. If the system does not know which parity sectors were being updated, it may have to regenerate **all** parity after restart. They therefore describe logging the consistent/inconsistent state of parity sectors to stable storage and regenerating those marked inconsistent after a crash.

This is a particularly useful counterexample to a simplistic material model of redundancy:

```text
parity sector physically present
        ≠
parity sector safely usable for reconstruction
```

The encoded redundancy relation has a currentness condition, and that condition can require retained metadata of its own.

**Primary anchor:** Chen et al., UCB/CSD-93-778, §3.5.2, PDF pp. 31–32.

### H/P — continued service with a failed disk is a distinct operational state from completed repair

Chen et al. explicitly discuss `Operating with a Failed Disk`. They warn that doing so is risky because a further system crash can destroy information needed by the already-degraded array.

Their first repair method, `demand reconstruction`, requires stand-by spare disks. Access to a parity stripe containing an invalid sector immediately reconstructs the required data onto a spare. Separately, a **background process scans the entire disk array** so that all contents of the failed disk are eventually reconstructed within an acceptable time.

The source therefore supplies two different completion conditions:

1. an individual request can be served despite the failed member;
2. the failed member’s contribution can later be fully reconstructed across the array.

Those are not the same retention state.

**Primary anchor:** Chen et al., UCB/CSD-93-778, §3.5.3, PDF pp. 32–33.

### H/P — spare-free repair can move the encoded relation and require additional meta state

Chen et al.’s second method, `parity sparing`, handles systems without stand-by spares. Before servicing a write that touches a stripe with an invalid sector, the invalid sector is reconstructed and relocated over the corresponding parity sector; the sector is marked relocated. When a replacement disk finally arrives, the relocated sector is copied, parity is regenerated, and the relocated mark is removed.

Thus even within one period source, `repair` is not one operation. It can involve a temporary change to where data/parity roles live, plus metadata identifying that altered relation.

**Primary anchor:** Chen et al., UCB/CSD-93-778, §3.5.3, PDF pp. 32–33.

### H/P — reconstruction time is part of the reliability boundary

Chen et al.’s reliability discussion explicitly treats additional failures during reconstruction as a threat. It highlights multiple/correlated disk failures and unrecoverable bit errors encountered while reconstructing as mechanisms that reduce array reliability.

This matters because a logical array can remain usable after the first failure while nevertheless occupying a more vulnerable temporal interval until reconstruction completes.

The source does not license one universal numerical rebuild deadline for all RAID. It establishes the mechanism-level point that **repair duration changes the risk envelope**.

**Primary anchor:** Chen et al., UCB/CSD-93-778, reliability discussion preceding §3.5, especially the discussion of failures during reconstruction and §3.4.6.

## Retained state

This case requires at least five distinct retention targets.

### 1. Current user data

The logical data presented by the array. Under a one-member failure, some requested logical contribution may have no surviving current material instance on its original disk.

### 2. Redundancy relation

The parity/checksum relation connecting multiple data contributions. In the bounded XOR/check-sum scheme, this relation makes one missing contribution reconstructable from the rest.

### 3. Validity/currentness meta state

Which logical sectors or stripe contributions are valid, invalid, consistent, inconsistent, or relocated. This prevents stale physical material from answering as current data or parity.

### 4. Reconstruction progress

The array may have rebuilt some of a failed member and not the rest. Chen et al. explicitly count reconstruction progress as `meta state` that must be maintained across crashes.

### 5. Repair capacity

Stand-by spare space or another place into which reconstructed data can be committed. The spare contains no user payload before it is used, but it is infrastructure that allows degraded reconstructability to be converted back into a less-degraded physical configuration.

---

## Substrate and coding relation

The bounded arrangement is not a single substrate. It is a relation across several physical disks:

```text
data contribution A
        +
data contribution B
        +
...
        +
check/parity contribution
        +
validity / repair meta state
        ↓
current logical stripe / record remains reconstructable
```

In Ouchi’s XOR example, losing one physical segment does not leave another full copy of that segment. The missing contribution is synthesized from the remaining record segments plus the check sum.

This sharply differs from simple replication:

```text
replication:
    another complete copy may survive

parity/checksum reconstruction:
    no surviving member need contain the complete missing contribution
    yet the contribution can be computed from the surviving coded group
```

This is an engineering comparison, not a claim that Ouchi used later `erasure coding` vocabulary.

## Retention mechanism

### Quiescent media retention

Individual magnetic disk sectors retain their ordinary physical state through the disk’s normal media mechanism. This case does not re-document magnetic-domain physics; detailed disk history belongs primarily in `computing-archaeology`.

### Encoded redundancy

The array additionally retains enough redundant relation to reconstruct one unavailable contribution under the bounded failure model.

### Failure detection and invalidation

The system must identify a failed member/contribution and stop stale surviving material from answering as current.

### On-demand reconstruction

A read or write that encounters an invalid contribution can trigger reconstruction from the surviving redundancy group.

### Background rebuild

Independent of immediate request service, a background process can reconstruct the remainder of the failed member onto spare capacity.

### Parity/currentness maintenance

Writes and crashes can make parity temporarily inconsistent. Correct reconstruction therefore depends not only on parity bytes but on whether they are known to correspond to the current data relation.

---

## Read semantics

The crucial read is a read whose normal physical member is missing or invalid.

Under the bounded parity relation, the array can reconstruct the requested logical contribution from surviving members. That reconstruction is not evidence that the failed sector still physically contains recoverable data. It is evidence that the **logical contribution remains derivable from the surviving coded relation**.

A second read boundary is equally important: a failed but intermittently readable disk may still hold bytes, yet Chen et al. require invalid marking precisely so that stale material does not answer. Readability and admissible currentness are separate.

---

## Write semantics

Writes must preserve both user data and a current redundancy relation.

Chen et al.’s crash discussion shows why these cannot be collapsed. An interrupted write can leave old/new data and parity out of relation even though each individual sector remains physically readable. The system must therefore track or regenerate parity consistency.

While operating with a failed disk, writes create still stricter obligations because the array has already consumed the ordinary one-member redundancy margin. Demand reconstruction or parity sparing changes where the current state is embodied before or while such writes continue.

## Time

This case adds several timescales to the repository:

- ordinary disk read/write time;
- failure-detection and invalidation time;
- one-request reconstruction time;
- time spent operating with a failed member;
- background reconstruction/rebuild duration;
- interval until replacement capacity is available;
- crash-recovery parity-regeneration time;
- time before another failure or unreadable surviving sector appears.

The most important distinction is:

> **A logical array can continue serving requests during a time interval in which its future failure tolerance is lower than before.**

Retention is therefore not merely `available / unavailable`. It can have a **repair frontier** and a changing failure margin while the logical object remains in service.

`repair frontier` and `degraded retention margin` are project terms, not historical quotations.

---

## Failure and forgetting modes

Distinct failures include:

- loss of a disk member while parity/checksum reconstruction still succeeds;
- a second relevant member failure before reconstruction restores redundancy;
- an unrecoverable read error in a surviving member needed for reconstruction;
- failure to mark the failed member’s logical sectors invalid, allowing stale data to answer;
- failure to mark reconstructed sectors valid/current at the correct boundary;
- parity bytes surviving physically but being inconsistent with current data after an interrupted write;
- loss/corruption of the meta state that records failed members or reconstruction progress;
- insufficient or unavailable spare capacity for the chosen repair method;
- crash during already-degraded operation, when the remaining recoverability relation is more fragile.

Do not collapse these into a single `disk failed, RAID saved it` narrative. Some failures consume redundancy without losing current data; others destroy the relation needed for reconstruction; others preserve reconstructability but prevent restoration of the previous failure margin.

## Engineering reconstruction

### E — parity redundancy ≠ replica multiplicity

The missing contribution can remain reconstructable without an intact duplicate anywhere. The retained target is partly an **encoded relation across surviving members**.

### E — service continuity ≠ restored redundancy margin

A request can succeed while a member remains failed. Full reconstruction onto replacement/spare capacity is a later retention-maintenance completion event.

### E — redundancy bytes ≠ redundancy currentness

A parity sector can be materially present but inconsistent after a crash-interrupted write. Validity/consistency metadata determines whether surviving redundancy may safely participate in reconstruction.

### E — repair progress can itself be constitutive state

Chen et al. explicitly include `how much of a failed disk has been reconstructed` in meta state. A system in the middle of rebuild is not adequately described only by current user payload plus an abstract RAID level.

### E — spare capacity can be retention infrastructure without being payload

An unused stand-by spare stores no current user object, yet it makes rapid reconstruction and restoration of fault margin possible. This parallels—but is not identical to—Case 15’s stored capacitor energy: infrastructure can be constitutive of future continuity without itself being the retained payload.

### E — reconstructability is failure-model relative

The bounded XOR/parity relation tolerates the missing contribution only under a specified surviving-group condition. The case does not support the universal claim that `RAID means data survives any disk failure`.

## Cross-case comparison

### Case 05 — RADOS replicated-object repair

Both cases can preserve a higher-level object while a physical member disappears and both can trigger repair after failure. But the recovery source differs:

- RADOS repair uses surviving object replicas plus placement/version/currentness rules;
- bounded parity RAID can synthesize a missing contribution from several surviving data/parity members even when no complete duplicate of the missing contribution exists.

So:

> **failure-triggered repair is a useful functional comparison; replica repair and parity reconstruction are not the same historical or engineering mechanism.**

### Case 14 — SCSI defect reassignment

Case 14 shows one LBA moving to a spare physical sector, while Seagate explicitly warns that the reassignment command does not itself preserve the old payload. Case 17 supplies a different recovery relation: an array may reconstruct missing payload algebraically from other members and then write it onto spare/replacement space.

So `spare substitution` is not enough to characterize retention. One must ask **where the replacement value comes from**.

### Case 16 — BSD FFS soft updates

Both Case 16 and Case 17 show that auxiliary currentness state matters even when payload sectors survive. But the relations are different:

- soft updates constrains which filesystem metadata combinations may become crash-admissible;
- RAID meta state constrains which disk-array contributions/parity are valid and how reconstruction proceeds.

This is a cross-layer analogy only, not a historical lineage.

## Philosophical interpretation

### I — retained identity can exceed the survival of its constituent embodiments

A logical contribution can continue to count as retained even after its original physical member is unavailable, provided the surviving coded relation can reconstruct an admissible continuation.

This strengthens the repository’s relational criterion: persistence may depend not on one token surviving but on a structured relation preserving enough constraint to **re-create what counts as the same current contribution**.

### I — repair changes the future of retention, not only the present

Rebuild is interesting philosophically because the current logical state may already be accessible. The maintenance work instead restores **future survivability**—the ability to withstand another failure under the intended redundancy regime.

This should not be inflated into a general ontology of care or memory. It is first an engineering fact about a changed failure margin.

### I — parity is not a “memory of the missing disk” in the historical sense

That phrase may be rhetorically tempting, but it obscures the mechanism. Parity/checksum is a coded relation from which one missing contribution can be reconstructed under specified conditions. The historical sources discuss redundancy, check sums, parity, failures, and reconstruction—not a philosophical memory faculty.

## Functional analogies and limits

### A — relational retention without one surviving token

Case 17 can be compared functionally to delay-line regeneration, destructive-read restore, DRAM refresh, mapped Flash relocation, and RADOS repair because logical continuity can survive replacement of immediate physical state.

But the mechanism is distinct in each case. Here the missing contribution is reconstructed from an explicit redundancy group.

### X — `RAID = backup`

Rejected. The bounded sources concern online redundant storage and reconstruction under component/system failures. They do not establish an independent historical backup copy with separate failure/administrative boundaries.

### X — `RAID = mirroring`

Rejected. The bounded case is specifically parity/checksum reconstruction where the missing contribution need not survive as a duplicate.

### X — `Patterson, Gibson, Katz invented parity disk recovery`

Rejected. Ouchi’s 1977-filed IBM patent predates the RAID name and directly describes XOR/check-sum reconstruction across failure-independent storage units.

### X — `successful degraded read means redundancy is fully restored`

Rejected. Chen et al. explicitly separate request-time reconstruction from background reconstruction of the entire failed disk.

### X — `parity physically present means parity is current`

Rejected. Chen et al. directly discuss parity becoming inconsistent after interrupted writes and requiring currentness state/regeneration.

### X — `this case grounds scrubbing`

Rejected. The bounded source set discusses reconstruction and parity consistency/reliability, but this case does not claim a dedicated periodic media-scrubbing regime. Scrubbing remains open work.

## Why this case is `grounded`

The central retention claims do not depend on a later textbook summary:

1. a 1977-filed IBM primary patent directly grounds XOR/check-sum reconstruction across failure-independent storage devices and controls invention-priority claims;
2. the 1987/1988 Berkeley source grounds the historical `RAID` name and level taxonomy;
3. the 1993/1994 Chen et al. period technical account directly grounds meta state, validity/currentness, parity inconsistency, demand reconstruction, stand-by spares, background reconstruction, and repair risk;
4. the mechanism, failure modes, maintenance work, and source boundaries are explicit;
5. functional comparison with RADOS and HDD remapping is labeled as comparison rather than genealogy;
6. related-repository duplication was checked;
7. dedicated scrubbing and later double-parity/rebuild policy remain explicitly outside the bounded claim.

The result is therefore strong enough for `grounded` status while remaining deliberately narrower than a comprehensive RAID history.
